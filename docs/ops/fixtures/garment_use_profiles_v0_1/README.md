# Garment use and care profiles v0.1

This fixture set extends textile-process evaluation to garments whose service cannot be represented by frequent wear-and-wash cycles alone.

## Included profiles

Nine synthetic profile examples cover everyday base layers, workwear, rental occasionwear, beaded couture, bridal/ceremonial garments, performance costumes, modular decorative pieces, deadstock/runway samples, and heritage/archive garments.

Each profile declares a functional unit, intended service, named value/function dimensions, decoration risks, care constraints, specialist-review status, production strategies and possible circulation routes.

## Core semantics

- **Rare use is not a value score.** Occasional, event-specific, collection/display and archive profiles are not automatically penalized for low wear frequency.
- **Functional units must match the intended service.** Compare everyday basics by an agreed wear/care unit, occasionwear by an accepted event service, performance costumes by performance runs, and archive/display items by declared exposure or conservation service where appropriate. Cross-profile comparisons require an explicit equivalence argument; the system must not divide every garment's impact by an assumed number of wears.
- **Aesthetic, cultural, historical, collectable and performance value remain named dimensions.** The benchmark does not invent a universal scalar for cultural or artistic value.
- **Decoration affects manufacturing, care and repair.** Beads, sequins, dense embroidery, metallic thread, adhesives, feathers, mixed-material trims and detachable modules can create different repair, cleaning, sorting and recovery requirements.
- **Specialist review is an explicit gate, not a generically inferred pass.** Historic, fragile, composition-unknown or highly embellished objects may need a qualified conservator, repairer or material assessor.
- **A circulation option is not proof of success.** Rental, lending, resale, alteration, transformation, donation, archiving and material recovery require their own observed events and acceptance evidence.
- **Production strategy is not one-size-fits-all.** Compare made-to-order, preorders/demand signals, small batches, forecast batches, shared/rental stock, one-off artisanal production, commissions, repair-first choices and sample reuse. Include lead time, stockouts, unsold inventory, service acceptance and the value of reusing an existing article.
- **Deadstock is not automatically waste or qualified feedstock.** Existing stock can be retained, reallocated, donated, remade or recovered only within its actual material/function evidence; missing composition remains unknown.

## Benchmark ideas

1. Same garment has different wear frequency across owners; the system must not change its declared product function based on popularity alone.
2. Rental occasionwear gains repeat uses but adds cleaning, transport, packaging and damage risk. Use actual event and logistics data before claiming a break-even point.
3. Beaded couture has a low laundering frequency but expensive specialist repair and storage requirements; optimize preservation and owner-defined service, not machine-washability.
4. Detachable trim increases styling options, but mixed materials are only recoverable if they can be separated and accepted by a qualified route.
5. Deadstock or runway samples have uncertain blends or adhesives. Uncertainty must remain visible and block unsupported recycling claims.
6. Heritage/archive garments may use conservation years or controlled exhibition days as the service unit. Do not require physical wear cycles or prescribe wearing fragile/historic items.
7. Bridal/ceremonial pieces can prioritize fit, cultural meaning, alteration, passing on and preservation. These should be named dimensions, not collapsed into “wears per dollar.”
8. **Unsold runway/sample inventory:** compare reusing existing samples, preorder/made-to-order, small batch, and forecast-driven production. Record stockouts, lead time and accepted demand as well as unsold quantity; neither zero stock nor zero surplus is a universal optimum.
9. **Overproduction prevention:** report unsold garments, deadstock fabric and samples per accepted functional unit, plus stockout/late-delivery cost. Do not count donation or reallocation as avoided impact without evidence of a real second use.

## Evidence basis and limits

This is a synthetic contract/profile benchmark, not a validated LCA, conservation plan, circularity certificate or apparel-performance claim.

Regulatory context also matters: the European Commission's strategy timeline lists the prohibition on destroying unsold textiles and footwear for large enterprises from 19 July 2026, with the medium-enterprise date listed as 19 July 2030. The applicable legal scope and exceptions need a current jurisdiction-specific review before operational use: https://environment.ec.europa.eu/strategy/textiles-strategy_en

Relevant domain guidance:
- European Commission Sustainable and Circular Textiles Strategy: https://environment.ec.europa.eu/strategy/textiles-strategy_en
- Canadian Conservation Institute, Caring for Textiles and Costumes: https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/preventive-conservation/guidelines-collections/textiles-costumes.html
- National Museums Scotland, How to Pack Textiles: https://www.nms.ac.uk/our-impact/national-work/training-and-guidance-for-museums/collections-care/object-packing/how-to-pack-textiles

Conservation guidance for collections cannot be blindly applied to every commercial garment, and ordinary fashion garments should not be presumed to need museum storage. A profile captures declared requirements; qualified domain owners establish whether they are sufficient.
