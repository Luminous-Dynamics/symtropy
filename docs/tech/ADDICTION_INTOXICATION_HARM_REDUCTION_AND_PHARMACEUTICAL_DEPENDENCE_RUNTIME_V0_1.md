# Addiction, Intoxication, Harm Reduction, and Pharmaceutical Dependence Runtime

## Purpose

Substance use in Symtropy may be medical, recreational, ritual, occupational, compulsive, coercive, or several at once.

The runtime must avoid both glamorization and moral simplification.

## Core Principle

> **A substance effect is biological and contextual. Addiction is a lived pattern involving body, access, stress, meaning, relationships, and institutions—not a vice meter.**

# 1. Substance Model

Each substance or intervention defines:

```text
active compounds or functional class
route of administration
onset and duration
therapeutic range
acute effects
impairments
interactions
tolerance profile
dependence potential
withdrawal profile
overdose or toxicity risks
storage and supply requirements
legal and cultural context
```

The game need not simulate pharmacology at clinical precision. It must preserve causal differences relevant to play.

# 2. Person State

A person's state may include:

- current exposure;
- expected effect;
- tolerance by substance class;
- physical dependence;
- craving intensity;
- withdrawal risk;
- pain and untreated symptoms;
- sleep debt;
- nutrition and hydration;
- stress and trauma cues;
- social setting;
- treatment plan;
- personal goals;
- prior adverse events;
- privacy scope.

No single `addicted` flag controls behavior.

# 3. Use Contexts

The same substance may be:

- prescribed medicine;
- self-medication;
- ritual sacrament;
- workplace stimulant;
- recreational drug;
- poison;
- coercive compliance tool;
- withdrawal prevention;
- black-market substitute;
- culturally ordinary food or drink.

Context does not erase physical effect. Physical effect does not determine moral meaning automatically.

# 4. Intoxication

Intoxication may alter:

- reaction time;
- balance;
- perception;
- memory formation;
- risk appraisal;
- emotional regulation;
- speech;
- pain;
- sleep;
- decision capacity.

It must not produce random personality replacement.

A kind person does not automatically become violent. A violent act is not excused solely by intoxication. Capacity, causation, responsibility, and safety remain separate questions.

# 5. Tolerance and Dependence

Tolerance develops through repeated exposure, physiology, dosing pattern, and time.

Physical dependence means abrupt reduction may cause withdrawal. It does not by itself establish addiction.

A patient taking long-term pain medication may be physically dependent while using it exactly as intended.

An addiction pattern involves persistent use despite meaningful harm, impaired control, or a narrowed life, interpreted through the person's own goals and circumstances.

# 6. Withdrawal

Withdrawal can be medically dangerous.

Possible effects include:

- pain;
- anxiety;
- tremor;
- sleep disruption;
- nausea;
- sensory changes;
- cognitive impairment;
- seizures or other acute danger for relevant substances;
- emotional distress;
- re-emergence of the condition the substance treated.

The game must not make forced withdrawal a universally safe punishment or instant cure.

# 7. Harm Reduction

Harm-reduction systems may provide:

- accurate substance information;
- testing for contamination;
- sterile equipment where relevant;
- overdose-response tools;
- hydration, food, and rest;
- supervised consumption settings;
- safer dosing guidance;
- medication-assisted treatment;
- withdrawal management;
- sober companions;
- quiet rooms;
- transport home;
- nonjudgmental medical care;
- peer support;
- privacy.

These systems reduce risk without requiring immediate abstinence.

They consume real labor, supplies, trust, space, and political support.

# 8. Treatment and Recovery

Treatment options may include:

- voluntary abstinence;
- moderated use;
- medication support;
- substitution therapy;
- pain treatment;
- trauma care;
- housing and income support;
- peer groups;
- spiritual practice;
- structured residential care;
- informal family support;
- changing work conditions.

Recovery is nonlinear.

Relapse may occur without erasing all prior progress. It is not triggered as a moral punishment for one wrong dialogue choice.

A person may define recovery differently from a clinician, family member, employer, or faction.

# 9. Pharmaceutical Dependence and Access

Societies may create dependence through medicine and infrastructure.

Examples:

- proprietary enzymes required by body modifications;
- anti-rejection medication;
- pain treatment;
- psychiatric medication;
- stimulant schedules for emergency crews;
- radiation-protection compounds;
- reproductive medicine;
- symbiont maintenance;
- machine coolant or cognitive substrate analogues.

Control over necessary medication can become labor, migration, domestic, or political coercion.

A missed supply shipment may create a public-health crisis rather than a market-price inconvenience.

# 10. Occupational Use

Some professions normalize substances:

- alertness compounds for long shifts;
- pain suppression for heavy labor;
- anti-nausea medication in altered gravity;
- emotional dampening in traumatic work;
- ritual intoxicants among caregivers or soldiers;
- performance enhancers in sport.

The game should expose institutional incentives:

- unsafe scheduling;
- underreported side effects;
- mandatory testing;
- employer control of treatment;
- workers hiding use to keep credentials;
- unions demanding safer staffing instead of chemical endurance.

# 11. Social Relationships

Substance use affects relationships through:

- trust;
- broken commitments;
- caregiving;
- financial strain;
- fear;
- shared ritual;
- pleasure;
- secrecy;
- stigma;
- codependency;
- boundary setting;
- reconciliation.

Family love does not guarantee safety. Refusing contact may be necessary. Helping does not require surrendering all boundaries.

# 12. Coercive Use

Institutions or individuals may use substances to control:

- workers;
- detainees;
- patients;
- soldiers;
- intimate partners;
- migrants;
- reconstituted people;
- machine or alien bodies with analogous dependencies.

Coercive patterns include:

- withholding necessary medicine;
- nonconsensual sedation;
- compulsory performance drugs;
- falsified treatment records;
- dosing tied to obedience;
- withdrawal used as punishment;
- chemical restraint for institutional convenience.

These are authority and rights violations, not merely health states.

# 13. Law and Enforcement

Different societies may criminalize, regulate, medicalize, commercialize, ritualize, or tolerate substances.

The simulation distinguishes:

- possession;
- use;
- impairment during risky work;
- sale;
- contamination;
- coercive distribution;
- trafficking;
- medical prescribing;
- public safety;
- private behavior.

Enforcement may create corruption, unsafe markets, discriminatory targeting, or barriers to care.

The game does not assume harsher punishment reduces harm.

# 14. NPC Behavior

Substance state may influence but not dictate behavior.

Decision systems receive grounded signals such as:

- impairment;
- craving;
- withdrawal urgency;
- pain;
- shame;
- fear of discovery;
- treatment commitment;
- relationship boundaries;
- available alternatives.

NPCs retain values, habits, responsibilities, and individual variation.

The model must not encode “addicted NPCs lie” or “intoxicated NPCs become violent” as universal rules.

# 15. Player Mechanics

Player substance use may affect:

- sensory presentation;
- motor performance;
- sleep;
- pain;
- memory reliability;
- social response;
- health;
- legal and occupational standing.

Controls:

- effects may be reduced or represented through UI alternatives;
- motion and visual distortion require accessibility options;
- withdrawal gameplay must remain bounded;
- no monetization may exploit craving-like loops;
- IRIS may warn and assist but cannot make treatment choices for the player.

# 16. Privacy

Substance-use and treatment records are private health information.

Disclosure may occur through:

- consent;
- direct observation;
- workplace safety process with limited scope;
- medical emergency;
- court order under local law;
- public self-disclosure;
- unlawful leak.

Rumor does not become diagnosis.

# 17. Content and Tone

The game may show:

- intoxication;
- overdose aftermath;
- withdrawal;
- relapse;
- family conflict;
- unsafe venues;
- harm-reduction care;
- recovery;
- ordinary moderate use;
- people who choose abstinence;
- people whose use remains complicated.

It should avoid:

- glamorous invulnerability;
- comic withdrawal;
- addiction as instant villain coding;
- one miracle treatment;
- mandatory tragic endings;
- using overdose solely to make another character sad;
- procedural instructions that facilitate real-world dangerous use.

# 18. Runtime Events

Representative events include:

```text
new exposure
expected dose
dose missed
contaminant detected
intoxication threshold crossed
capacity concern
withdrawal risk
medical emergency
harm-reduction intervention
treatment started
treatment changed
relapse
boundary set
supply interruption
coercive withholding suspected
recovery milestone
```

Events carry provenance and privacy scope.

# 19. Acceptance Failures

The runtime fails if:

- addiction is one morality stat;
- intoxication rewrites personality randomly;
- withdrawal is universally harmless;
- treatment success depends on obedience to one ideology;
- pain patients are automatically treated as addicts;
- private use becomes globally known;
- relapse erases all progress;
- coercive dosing is treated as ordinary management;
- harm reduction is framed as enabling failure;
- disabled visual effects are required to understand state;
- player substance mechanics imitate manipulative monetization loops.

## Closing Principle

> **The mature question is not whether a substance is good or bad. It is what the substance does, why the person uses it, who controls access, what harms are occurring, and what choices remain genuinely available.**
