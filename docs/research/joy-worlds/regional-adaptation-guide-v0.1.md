## Governing principle

Build **one reusable evidence and simulation platform, not one exported destination template**.

A successful destination in one region might be a high-energy water park with excellent indoor storm shelter; elsewhere it might be a dry, night-oriented desert attraction; another site might be a community-run coastal network; an alpine resort may need to diversify beyond winter; a cruise itinerary must fit the actual capacity of each port and host community.

The site and its people own the context. A broad climate or culture label may suggest questions, but it cannot fill in local measurements, permit decisions, community consent or engineering coefficients.

## The shared-local architecture

| Shared across destinations | Must be local/site specific |
|---|---|
| Versioned scenario manifests and evidence provenance | Actual site boundary, terrain, ecology and hazard survey |
| Reproducible paired comparisons, seed/control semantics and outcome reporting | Climate baselines, extremes, future scenarios and uncertainty |
| Queue/service-flow accounting and independent oracles | Attraction mix, staffing rates, transport patterns and demand |
| Multi-objective reporting and explicit infeasibility | Legal limits, safety envelope and community-adopted constraints |
| Privacy, consent, retention and disclosure design patterns | Languages, cultural authority, traditions, customary access, local rights and appropriate stories |
| Reusable water/energy/material accounting structures | Source quality, availability, infrastructure capacities and local accounting boundary |
| Shared incident/closure vocabulary | Responsible authorities, evacuation, rescue, communications and recovery plans |
| Interfaces for commitments and verified outcome claims | Suppliers, workforce, ownership, benefit-sharing and distributional goals |

## Global context atlas

The following are question-generating archetypes, not deterministic country categories. A single country can contain several different profiles, and one site may combine many.

### 1. Tropical islands and coral coasts

**Experience possibilities:** reef and ocean discovery, community-led marine culture, shade-rich gardens, beach play, paddling, local food and craft, stargazing, sheltered indoor storm alternatives.

**Design questions:** How do storm surge, erosion, reef condition, freshwater supply, wastewater and ferry/air access shape feasible operations? Can the development preserve customary access and the community's other uses of the shore? Which experiences are possible without disturbing wildlife or implying guaranteed sightings?

**Red lines:** no siting decision based on a generic “tropical paradise” profile; no assumption that a reef or mangrove is adequate protection without local engineering/ecological analysis; no claim of local prosperity without value-flow evidence.

### 2. Arid and water-stressed regions

**Experience possibilities:** night gardens, astronomy, desert geology, shaded adventure, immersive storytelling, craft/food, low-water play, indoor dry experiences, cooling/rest spaces.

**Design questions:** What water source is available under drought and competing local needs? Which hours are safe for visitors and staff? How much energy does cooling require? What happens during dust storms, grid outages or flash floods?

**Red lines:** do not include large water features by default. Treat potable demand and water rights as constraints. Desalination, wastewater reuse and high-efficiency cooling still require lifecycle and safety evidence.

### 3. Mediterranean, subtropical and hot-summer coasts

**Experience possibilities:** heritage/food-led shoulder season, shaded promenades, early/late-day outdoor programming, coastal ecology, evening festivals and water-sensitive gardens.

**Design questions:** What happens through multi-day heatwaves, drought, wildfire smoke or water restrictions? Can night noise be managed for residents? Is historic infrastructure and transport able to support peak attendance?

**Red lines:** weather and beach conditions are not guaranteed; heritage access and public shore access are not disposable to increase throughput.

### 4. Monsoon and high-rainfall regions

**Experience possibilities:** rain-season culture, covered walkways, forest interpretation, craft/food spaces, wetlands where suitable, storm-resilient indoor programming.

**Design questions:** How does the catchment react to extreme rain? Where are flood-safe refuges? What are the risks from slopes, drainage, humidity, corrosion, transport cuts and contaminated recreation water?

**Red lines:** drainage does not automatically make a site safe; vegetation does not guarantee slope stability; closure and reopening criteria require responsible authorities.

### 5. Temperate maritime and variable-weather regions

**Experience possibilities:** surf and coastal adventure, indoor/outdoor hybrids, galleries/maker districts, festival gardens, food tourism, walkable waterfronts and compact regional parks.

**Design questions:** Which experiences remain enjoyable in wind and rain? How does visitor demand change by season? What is the lifecycle trade-off between new construction and adaptive reuse? How will public transport and residents absorb event peaks?

### 6. Alpine and snow-dependent regions

**Experience possibilities:** skiing and snow play when conditions permit, plus year-round mountain culture, hiking, climbing, cycling where appropriate, gastronomy, wellness, learning and observatories.

**Design questions:** Under what local temperature and water conditions is snowmaking viable? What are its energy, water and ecological costs? How can a resort retain workers and local services outside peak season? How do avalanche, heat, fire, landslide and transport risks alter the operating envelope?

**Red lines:** a snowmaking cannon is not a climate strategy by itself. Do not project long-run viability from a single snowy winter or replace ski monoculture with an equally disruptive mass-season alternative.

### 7. Boreal, subarctic and polar/expedition settings

**Experience possibilities:** small-group natural-history learning, dark-sky and seasonal phenomena, local cultural experience, warm shelters, modular facilities and guided expeditions.

**Design questions:** How far away is professional rescue? What are fuel/logistics/waste burdens? What seasonal wildlife exclusions are needed? What happens during loss of power, communications or transport?

**Red lines:** no promised aurora or wildlife sightings; no remote tourism without a realistic rescue and waste-removal plan; local and Indigenous authority, knowledge and consent are non-optional where relevant.

### 8. Savanna and semi-arid wildlife/cultural landscapes

**Experience possibilities:** community-led guiding, geology, astronomy, food/craft, low-impact wildlife observation and conservation interpretation.

**Design questions:** What are local water priorities and seasonal wildlife movements? Who controls land and visitor access? Where does tourism revenue go? How do vehicles, noise and guest behaviour affect wildlife?

**Red lines:** no guaranteed wildlife encounter; conservation branding is not evidence of conservation outcomes.

### 9. Freshwater lake, river and wetland destinations

**Experience possibilities:** paddling, non-invasive wildlife observation, waterside trails, local food/fishing culture where permitted, watershed learning and seasonal recreation.

**Design questions:** What is the entire basin's water balance? Who uses the water upstream/downstream? What are flood, drought, bloom, contamination and breeding-season constraints?

**Red lines:** visible water is not proof of sustainable supply or safe bathing; no attraction operation should bypass water-quality closure authority.

### 10. Dense cities and peri-urban locations

**Experience possibilities:** compact immersive theatres, maker spaces, rooftops, mixed-use leisure, gardens, food, public art and all-weather indoor/outdoor experiences.

**Design questions:** Can transit carry peaks? What are resident noise and light constraints? Are public spaces and service systems overloaded? Does reuse or new build have a better assessed lifecycle outcome?

**Red lines:** do not assume more events equal more community benefit; track housing, transport, public infrastructure and resident impacts.

## Beach resorts: from beachfront property to coastal stewardship

### Paradigm shift

The asset is not merely the hotel plus its private view. It is a changing coastal system that includes beach morphology, sediments, water, habitats, local access, livelihoods, hazards and long-term adaptation options.

The design profile should compare:
- nature-based interventions where locally appropriate and evaluated;
- hybrid or conventional engineering where qualified experts identify a need;
- development setbacks, reversible uses and retreat options;
- water supply and wastewater options with local priorities and lifecycle accounting;
- off-beach experiences during storms, heat, unsafe water or ecological recovery periods;
- visitor caps and route alternatives for sensitive environments;
- local access, cultural continuity, employment and supplier value retained.

Do not assume one protective solution works on every coast. IPCC and NOAA sources emphasize both potential benefits and limits/need for context-specific performance evaluation. [IPCC AR6 coastal ecosystems](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/chapter/chapter-3/) · [NOAA nature-based coastal solutions](https://coastalscience.noaa.gov/science-areas/climate-change/restoration-and-nature-based-solutions/).

## Cruise ships: from floating destination to port-connected mobility system

### Paradigm shift

Evaluate a cruise as a sequence of coupled systems: ship, fuel and hotel load, route and speed, berth, shore power, port city, shore excursions, waste/wastewater service, visitor arrival pulse and local value retention.

The regional profile must check:
- actual shore-power availability, compatibility and grid mix for each port;
- vessel and port energy/resource boundaries;
- emissions by stated lifecycle and operational boundary;
- port and destination capacity, transit, heritage sites and crowd peaks;
- local operator terms, cultural rights, accessibility and supplier value;
- weather diversions, missed ports and substitute experiences;
- onboard crowding and experience balance without identity surveillance;
- waste, wastewater, air pollution and underwater noise evidence.

## Ski resorts: from snow-only business to evidence-led mountain destination

### Paradigm shift

Snow reliability becomes one uncertain product input, not the single unquestioned operating premise. The destination may add year-round offerings, but only when the local ecology, access, safety, workforce and market evidence support them.

Compare natural-snow scenarios, snowmaking feasibility, storage, water and electricity availability, run grooming/management, trail recovery, lift operation and alternative seasonal programming as separate models. If a component lacks a qualified model, mark it unknown. Do not let the optimizer trade away water rights, habitat constraints or avalanche safety to keep a slope open.

A peer-reviewed review published online on 23 September 2026 synthesizes falling snow-cover duration/reliability and limitations in evidence about the effectiveness of ski-tourism adaptation. [Yang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.accre.2026.09.013). UNEP's Mountains ADAPT programme emphasizes locally driven and ecosystem-based adaptation. [UNEP Mountains ADAPT](https://www.unep.org/en/what-we-do/climate-action/adaptation/ecosystem-based-adaptation/mountains-adapt).

A second September 2026 study using a choice experiment with South Korean ski tourists found that more specialized skiers preferred spatial adaptation more strongly, while less-specialized tourists placed greater emphasis on operational reliability and convenience. Treat this as evidence that adaptation preferences can differ by segment—not as a universal rule for all countries or guests. [Choi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdmm.2026.101118).

**Maladaptation check:** snowmaking or infrastructure expansion might protect short-term operations while increasing water/energy demand, emissions, fixed costs and future lock-in. Compare at least natural-snow-only, targeted technical adaptation, four-season diversification, and managed-transition scenarios. Include a no-new-investment or reversible investment option where appropriate.

## Shared engineering workflow

1. Create a place dossier with precise site boundary, authoritative maps/data, cultural and customary access, climate and hazard sources, infrastructure, ecology, law and community review.
2. Define hard constraints first: safety, rights, water, habitat, access obligations, applicable legal requirements and emergency response.
3. Build a synthetic scenario only where the required real data is missing. Label every synthetic input.
4. Compare candidate layouts/portfolios from the same initial state, changing one declared intervention at a time.
5. Use independent accounting oracles for water, energy/material flows, queues or other tractable subsystems.
6. Preserve negative controls, unknowns, null/adverse outcomes, model disagreement and code/model identities.
7. Calibrate against observations under a frozen plan; separate calibration from held-out assessment.
8. Review results with local communities, the responsible authorities and qualified discipline experts.
9. Promote only the claim that the evidence actually supports.

## Recommended initial pilots

Do not pick final global winners before site data. Compare a few genuinely different contexts to test transferability:

- **Coastal pilot:** one beach destination with water, shoreline, habitat and public-access constraints.
- **Maritime pilot:** one hypothetical cruise itinerary that binds ship profile to several ports, shore-power availability, excursion capacities and local value flow.
- **Mountain pilot:** one ski area with natural-snow and snowmaking scenarios plus alternatives for low-snow conditions.
- **Contrast pilot:** one water-stressed arid site to test whether our shared contracts can represent different feasible portfolio decisions.

Use synthetic profiles first, then collect the local evidence required for a real pilot. A site profile cannot be certified by a schema check.

## Exit criterion

The same technical/evidence contract can express materially different beach, cruise, ski and water-stressed contexts, and the comparison runner rejects candidates that violate declared hard constraints. A reviewer can inspect local evidence needs, authorities, unknowns and closure policies for each profile. No project is called sustainable, resilient or superior merely because its profile validates.
