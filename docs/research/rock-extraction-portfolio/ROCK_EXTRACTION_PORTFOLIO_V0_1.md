# Rock Extraction Portfolio v0.1 — hard-rock methods, hybrid fracture and ancient quarrying

**Parent benchmark:** https://github.com/Luminous-Dynamics/symtropy/issues/1952  
**Related owners:** #1588, #1593, #1594, #1595, #1933, #3305  
**Status:** research synthesis and experimental programme; not a qualified rock-breaking model  
**Primary test materials:** characterized granite and basalt cohorts  
**Claim boundary:** method-specific bench and pilot results only; no claim of commercial readiness or safe field operating procedure follows from this document

## 1. Executive finding

We should investigate plasma, electrical pulses, sonics, ultrasound, thermal spallation, millimetre waves, microwaves, waterjets, cryogenic preconditioning and expansive chemical splitting—but not as one undifferentiated category of "novel excavation".

The strongest near-term strategy is **selective hybrid weakening followed by conventional mechanical removal**:

1. Characterize the actual rock and its stress/moisture/texture state.
2. Identify whether a local preconditioning method is physically coupled to that rock.
3. Deliver energy only where it can create useful cracks or kerfs.
4. Use mechanical cutters/drills to detach and size the resulting fragments.
5. Measure spoil recovery, fragment distribution, cooling, ventilation, wear, stoppages and full-cycle energy.
6. Select the method only where the complete system beats a matched mechanical baseline.

A technology that reduces bit force but creates more fines, heat, downtime, conversion losses or material-handling difficulty can be worse overall. The unit of comparison remains accepted full-cycle excavation, not a laboratory crack or a cutting-only energy ratio.

## 2. Terminology: distinguish the mechanisms

"Plasma", "sonic", "ultrasonic", "microwave" and "thermal" can refer to substantially different apparatus and physics.

| Research lane | Energy coupling / physical mechanism | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| High-voltage plasma-channel / electrohydraulic discharge (EHD) | Electrical breakdown in a fluid/rock-electrode system; a plasma channel and rapid pressure/shock response can initiate fractures | Not a plasma torch and not necessarily a continuous electric-arc cutting head |
| Electric-discharge / plasma-arc pulse (ED) | Electrical discharge and arc expansion; thermal and mechanical effects depend on apparatus and working fluid | Not interchangeable with EHD |
| Thermal plasma torch | Hot ionized gas transfers intense heat; potential thermal cracking, melting or vaporization | Not the millimetre-wave gyrotron method |
| Millimetre-wave / gyrotron drilling | High-power millimetre-wave electromagnetic energy heats, spalls, melts or vaporizes rock without a conventional downhole cutting bit | Not a plasma torch; the gyrotron is the power source |
| Microwave preconditioning | Dielectric heating, differential mineral absorption and thermal stress weaken rock before mechanical cutting | Not automatically a borehole ablation system |
| Thermal spallation | Flame/heat flux creates temperature gradients and grain-scale thermal stresses; flakes/spalls detach | Not bulk melting and not equivalent to laser ablation |
| Laser-assisted cutting/ablation | Concentrated optical energy heats, fractures, melts or vaporizes a localized region | Not simply a conventional disc cutter with an optical sensor |
| Sonic drilling | Longitudinal resonance/vibration of the drill string combined with bit contact and often rotation/percussion | Not necessarily ultrasound above 20 kHz |
| Ultrasonic-assisted drilling/cutting | High-frequency transducer-driven mechanical oscillation/impact at the tool-rock interface | Not acoustic-emission sensing and not ordinary sonic drill-string resonance |
| Acoustic emission (AE) | Passive sensing of transient elastic waves generated by cracking/friction | A diagnostic modality, not a rock-breaking technology |
| Active ultrasound | An emitted pulse and its reflected/transmitted response are used to interrogate material | Not the same as ultrasonic-assisted cutting |
| Abrasive/high-pressure waterjet | A coherent high-speed fluid jet and/or abrasive particles create kerfs, erosion and local fracture; often used to assist mechanical cutting | Not necessarily capable of detaching large hard-rock fragments alone |
| High-voltage-pulse mineral liberation | Dielectric breakdown preferentially fragments mineral/rock interfaces in particulate feed to liberate valuable phases or pre-weaken material before comminution | Not the same as in-situ plasma pre-cracking, and not a tunnel-face excavation method |
| Cryogenic / liquid-nitrogen preconditioning | Rapid cooling and thermal stress promote existing/new cracks and reduce strength under suitable conditions | Not a demonstrated continuous TBM replacement |
| Expansive chemical splitting | A cementitious/chemical expansion agent generates slow tensile stress in confined boreholes | Not explosive blasting and not a continuous face-cutting process |
| Mechanical percussion / disc cutting / rotary drilling | Direct impact, indentation, crushing, chipping and crack coalescence | The reference baseline, not an obsolete technology |

Symtropy #1594 already distinguishes AE diagnostics, active ultrasound and ultrasonic-assisted cutting. Preserve that distinction in the implementation.

## 3. Evidence and maturity map

The levels below are a qualitative evidence ladder for this research programme, not a formal TRL or certification:

- **R0 — concept:** plausible mechanism / proposal only.
- **R1 — analytical or small specimen:** theory, simulation or initial laboratory result.
- **R2 — controlled experimental fixture:** repeatable laboratory apparatus and measured rock response.
- **R3 — field/pilot evidence:** borehole, mine, quarry or TBM pilot; the full deployment boundary may still be incomplete.
- **R4 — sustained production:** repeated operation over useful duty cycles with full-cycle energy, maintenance, reliability and acceptance evidence.
- **R5 — independent transfer:** replicated by independent parties across sites, machines and relevant geology.

| Method / family | Evidence observed | Current posture for granite/basalt excavation | Major unresolved barriers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mechanical disc cutters, roadheaders, rotary/percussive drilling, drill-and-blast | Established industrial methods with extensive field use | R4–R5 as broad industrial families; exact machine/ground combinations still need declared envelopes | Wear, high thrust/torque, vibration, overbreak, support, blast restrictions, downtime and mucking bottlenecks |
| Axial–torsional coupled percussion (ATCP) | A 2026 hot-dry-rock study combines axial and torsional impacts; a Fushen-1 altered-granite interval above 180°C is compared with an adjacent conventional-rotary interval | R3 field evidence for a specific geothermal drilling configuration; adjacent intervals are not a randomized matched trial | Geology/interval confounding, transfer to basalt and ordinary-temperature granite, tool life, fluid/pump energy and full-cycle cost |
| Laser-assisted PDC cone-hybrid cutting | 2025 experimental/simulation study reports optimized cone-hybrid cutter geometry in granite with reduced comprehensive specific fragmentation energy | R1–R2 for the published granite configuration comparison | Laser source/beam delivery, cooling and facility energy, cutter wear, field durability and transfer to a tunnel face |
| Oscillating disc cutter (ODC) | Full-scale linear cutting rig study tests kinematic settings on red sandstone; paper also reports ODC machine use in a Sydney underground passage | R2 for the sandstone experiment; reported R3 machine deployment evidence for an application, not a granite/basalt performance dataset | High thrust/torque at stronger oscillation, drive/shaft fatigue, fragment-size versus cutting-energy trade-off and hard-rock transfer |
| High-pressure waterjet-assisted mechanical cutting | Laboratory and field/industrial TBM studies exist. A 2023 study reports field testing, but also identifies heating of tunnel air/water and loss of cutterhead cooling as long-term concerns. A separate granite laboratory study found its jet kerf alone did not produce independent large-fragment detachment | R3 for specific assist configurations; not a universal standalone hard-granite method | Nozzle/seal durability, clogging, pump power, water, heating/cooling, slurry and fragment liberation |
| Low-energy plasma-channel / EHD / electric-discharge pre-cracking (SPARC) | A 2025 granite study reports underwater pulsed discharges (~80 J/pulse), microcracks extending up to 9.4 mm and up to 56% lower specific cutting energy before a conventional PDC cutter; cracking was also demonstrated at elevated pressure (~300 atm) | R2 for granite pre-cracking plus mechanical cutting in a controlled drilling test; not a mine-scale full-cycle result | Reproducible mechanism attribution, matched specimens, energy boundary, electrode wear, fluid effects, scale-up and downhole integration |
| High-energy pulsed thermal plasma jet | A 2024 granite study compares pulsed and constant plasma jets; it reports 54.74% lower specific energy, 17.81% higher penetration rate and 50.06% lower electrode wear in its experimental comparison. A 2025 thermal study describes spalling-to-melting transitions and heat-affected regions | R2 for a laboratory granite drilling system; this is direct thermal removal and must not be pooled with low-energy SPARC pre-cracking | Full energy/power/cooling boundary, spall-versus-melt efficiency, electrode life under duty cycle, heat-affected damage and field-scale debris clearance |
| Thermal spallation / flame-jet assist | ETH Zurich has modelled the process; a thermo-mechanical combination has laboratory work and a field proof-of-concept on a conventional drill rig | R2–R3 for drilling-scale research, not a production tunnel face | Heat flux and thermal efficiency, rock-dependent spall response, heat-affected zone, fire/ventilation, debris clearing and confined-stress transfer |
| Gyrotron millimetre-wave drilling | MIT/MITEI reported a 118 m field hole in granite in 2025 and up to 5 m/h at that demonstration, based on company field testing. This is field evidence for a small-bore drilling application, not proof of large-diameter tunnel excavation economics | R3 for a specific granite borehole demonstration; not R4 for tunnels/mines | Borehole diameter, beam delivery, waveguide/launcher, vapour/dust clearing, power conversion/cooling, deep-hole propagation, formation variability and scale-up |
| Microwave preconditioning | Laboratory studies and a 2026 review cover thermal/electromagnetic damage and assisted cutter performance; some results show reductions in subsequent cutting forces/energy for particular granite tests | R1–R2 for a transferable method; individual test configurations may be more mature | Dielectric variability, mineral/grain-scale heating, reflection/penetration depth, combustible environments, shielding, heat management and whole-system energy |
| Laser-assisted rock cutting/ablation | Laboratory research and conceptual excavation diagrams exist; no comparable large-scale granite/basalt tunnel production evidence established in this scan | R1–R2 | Optical power at the face, beam delivery, dust/plume shielding, heat-affected damage, wall quality, efficiency and maintenance |
| Sonic drill-string resonance | Sonic drilling is used in subsurface sampling; a theoretical/analytical rock-breaking literature exists. One published study models flexible sonic-string percussive drilling; this is not a demonstrated hard-granite tunnel-production benchmark | R3 for some drilling/sampling applications; R1 for a transferable hard-rock continuous-excavation claim | Resonance stability with depth, drill-string coupling, amplitude at bit, energy lost in string, bit wear, confined rock response and scale |
| Ultrasonic impact-assisted drilling | A 2025 prototype paper reports stable 43 μm transducer amplitude and speed gains on sandstone and limestone; its abstract does not demonstrate granite or basalt performance | R2 for a specific device/material set; R1 for hard granite/basalt until tested | Transducer heat/fatigue, resonance detuning, downhole power transfer, contact coupling, bit life and rock-specific response |
| Liquid-nitrogen preconditioning | A 2025 controlled experiment reports that exposure time matters: about one minute increased microporosity without significant strength change; around ten minutes produced macrocracking and strength reductions; 60 minutes caused loss of load-bearing capacity in the tested sample | R2 for specimen damage; not a field excavation system | Cryogen production/transport/boil-off, thermal penetration, water content, stress state, cold safety, full-cycle energy and local applicability |
| Expansive chemical agents / soundless cracking | Established products and research for controlled static splitting; reviews cover hydration-driven expansion and in-situ fragmentation | R3–R4 for selected quarry/secondary-splitting contexts; not a continuous face machine | Borehole pattern, waiting time, temperature sensitivity, chemical residues, water demand, product consistency and throughput |
| Abrasive slurry/sawing and abrasive waterjet | Longstanding stone-cutting methods; abrasive is the cutting agent for many saw/drill systems, while the metal tool guides/transmits force | R4 for stone processing in suited geometries; use-case differs from bulk tunnel advance | Abrasive consumption/recovery, kerf width, wastewater/slurry, wear and productivity at large excavation scale |

### Important reading of the gyrotron result

MIT/MITEI reported Quaise's 118 m granite field hole and an up-to-5 m/h drilling rate in 2025. Treat this as a credible public demonstration of a novel *small-bore drilling process*, while recognizing the public account includes company performance claims and is not a peer-reviewed independent multi-site study. It does not answer whether the technology can economically remove a 5–15 m tunnel face, deal with heterogeneous ground at depth, clear vaporized material and maintain a safe, reliable production cycle.

### Important additions to mechanical and hybrid SOTA (November 2026)

**Axial–torsional coupled percussion is a stronger field lead than the phrase "sonic drilling" suggests.** Sun et al. report a high-temperature granite Fushen-1 test interval at 4,604–4,637 m, with 33 m drilled in 6.20 h (5.32 m/h), compared with an adjacent 4,653–4,824 m conventional rotary interval (164 m in 85 h, 1.93 m/h). The paper reports 175.65% higher ROP and 63.68% lower MSE for the ATCP interval. The laboratory programme used high-temperature granite, triaxial confinement, high-speed imaging and axial/torsional impact modes. The test is significant, but its adjacent-interval comparator is not a randomized matched control; it must be stored as observational field evidence, not as a causal effect estimate. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/S1876-3804(26)60774-4.

**Laser-assisted PDC cutter geometry is a separate hybrid lane.** Ma et al. report a 2025 cone-hybrid configuration with 70.81% lower comprehensive specific fragmentation energy against the paper's minimum mechanical baseline and 7.4% lower than a laser-assisted triple-cylinder configuration. Do not call the 70.81% a whole-rig energy or field productivity gain. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geoen.2025.214317.

**Oscillating disc cutters are a promising mechanical kinematic alternative.** Wang et al. use a full-scale linear cutting rig on red sandstone (UCS 61.33 MPa). Oscillation ratio and cutting depth affect thrust, torque, cutting specific energy and fragment size; in the tested range, stronger oscillation increased thrust/torque/cutting SE while reducing calculated downstream crushing energy by producing larger chips. The paper reports the Komatsu MC51 completed a Sydney underground passage in 2025, but that deployment statement is not a published hard-granite performance dataset. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/01445987261471924.

**Full-scale disc abrasion testing is a metrology lead.** Nasrabadi et al. report 12 FDAT tests on 305 mm discs in quartz-rich marble (UCS about 33.1 MPa; CAI 2.2), with specific cutter weight loss 0.14–2.09 mg/m. The study shows why wear needs load, speed and friction measurements; its tested rock is not hard granite. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tust.2026.107672.

## 4. Separate excavation from mineral liberation

High-voltage pulse fragmentation deserves a separate processing lane. It applies electrical pulses to particulate ore/rock to exploit dielectric-breakdown differences and preferentially break along mineral boundaries, with potential to improve liberation or pre-concentration before downstream grinding. A 2021 review describes mineral liberation, pre-weakening and pre-concentration applications, while a 2025 review focuses on discharge parameters and the effects of heterogeneity and pulse waveform. Both emphasize unresolved industrial barriers such as specific energy, equipment wear, water processing and throughput. This is not the same apparatus or objective as SPARC plasma pre-cracking ahead of a PDC bit, and it should be evaluated on feed preparation, liberated-grade/recovery, energy per tonne and downstream grinding energy—not metres per hour. Sources: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.powtec.2021.09.034 ; https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mineng.2025.109356.

## 5. What the mechanisms imply for granite versus basalt

"Granite" and "basalt" are families, not single standard materials.

A granite cohort can vary in quartz/feldspar/biotite proportions, grain size, fabric, alteration, moisture, pre-existing cracks and abrasivity. A basalt cohort can vary in grain size, vesicles, alteration, amygdales, joints and weathering. We must characterize the actual rock rather than assign a universal strength or universal response.

Useful hypotheses to test—not assumptions to hard-code:

1. **Thermal / microwave / laser methods:** differential expansion and dielectric/thermal properties of the mineral grains may create useful intergranular stress in some coarse-textured granites. A fine-grained, relatively homogeneous basalt may respond differently. Test rather than assume.
2. **Electrical-pulse methods:** electrical conductivity, dielectric breakdown path, saturation, pore fluid and discontinuity geometry may dominate over a simple UCS ranking. A more porous or wet specimen is not automatically easier or harder; the actual pulse path must be measured.
3. **Sonic/ultrasonic methods:** acoustic impedance, wave speed, bit contact stiffness, resonance and confinement matter. High-frequency energy at a transducer is irrelevant if it does not reach the bit-rock interface as intended.
4. **Waterjets:** kerf geometry, grain/boundary crack paths, pressure, standoff, flow, angle and confinement determine whether the jet merely grooves the face or enables disc-cutter crack coalescence.
5. **Cryogenic methods:** water content, initial temperature, permeability, thermal diffusivity and stress state affect how far and how rapidly cracks develop.
6. **Mechanical baseline:** UCS alone does not predict performance. Include fracture toughness, tensile strength, abrasivity, grain size, joint spacing, confinement, cutter geometry and fragment-size requirements.

For each specimen cohort, record petrography/mineralogy, grain-size distribution, density, porosity, moisture/saturation, UCS, Brazilian tensile strength, fracture toughness where available, Cerchar abrasivity where relevant, P/S wave velocity, thermal conductivity/diffusivity, heat capacity, electrical conductivity, dielectric properties (frequency- and temperature-specific), pre-existing discontinuities and confining stress.

## 6. The paradigm shift: localize the weakening, then let the best removal method do its job

A promising architecture is not "replace every cutter with plasma" or "heat the entire face with microwaves".

It is a selective weakening and extraction system:

`characterize rock -> infer local fracture response and uncertainty -> choose a qualified local assist -> create/extend a controlled kerf or microfracture zone -> mechanically detach -> classify and extract fragments -> measure accepted output`

Candidate hybrid pairings for controlled research:

- **Waterjet kerf + disc cutter:** kerf geometry creates a free surface / crack path; disc cutters remain responsible for large-fragment detachment.
- **Thermal-spall preconditioning + mechanical bit:** thermal stress weakens a local target, while a conventional bit removes the material.
- **Microwave preconditioning + mechanical cutting:** selective dielectric heating before a mechanical pass; only where measured dielectric coupling makes the power cost worthwhile.
- **Plasma/electrical pulse + mechanical cutting:** use a qualified pulse only if it creates a measurable, spatially useful damage zone and the complete electrical/electrode/fluid burden is acceptable.
- **Ultrasonic/vibro-impact + rotary/percussive drilling:** resonance-enhanced contact at the bit, not whole-face acoustic pulverization.
- **Cryogenic preconditioning + mechanical cutting:** investigate only if useful damage depth and total cryogen duty cycle beat mechanical-only operation.
- **Expansive splitting + mechanical handling:** possible for block/quarry/secondary fragmentation tasks with long dwell times, not a presumptive continuous TBM replacement.

The selector must be able to choose **no assist** when an assist's full-cycle cost is worse. Ordinary mechanical cutting may be the best method for a particular rock/geometry/production target.

## 7. Ancient hard-stone extraction: what the evidence says

Ancient hard-stone extraction is a useful engineering research topic because tool marks, quarry landscapes, residues and experimental reconstruction constrain what was physically done. It does not require a lost-technology explanation.

### 6.1 Granite at Aswan

- The Aswan Unfinished Obelisk and nearby quarries preserve extraction channels, depressions and unfinished work surfaces.
- Dolerite pounders/mauls are the best-established hard-stone quarrying tools in the archaeological record. Dolerite is tough and suitable for repeated percussive contact; its fracture mechanics can create localized crushing/chipping in granite.
- A 2023 photogrammetric experiment measured approximately **216 cm³/hour** of granite removed with a 3.47 kg dolerite pounder at 85 hits/minute (reported accuracy ±4.3 cm³ for the measured volume). Its authors concluded this was slower than older estimates and that pounders alone may not explain every aspect of large-scale quarrying.
- A 2025 fracture-mechanics paper further analyzes the suitability of dolerite pounders relative to granite. It supports the physical plausibility of the tool choice; it does not by itself establish the entire ancient workflow.
- The Metropolitan Museum of Art reports a more specific result: SEM-EDS analysis of residue in a tubular drill hole in an Amarna indurated-limestone fragment identified abundant corundum grains mixed with bronze corrosion products and powdered stone. The authors infer a bronze tubular drill used with a corundum-rich abrasive mixture for that object. This is direct evidence for that sample—not proof that the same abrasive was used for every granite monument or at Aswan. The origin and wider use of such abrasives remain open research questions.

### 6.2 Basalt and stone-tool quarrying

A 2016 Journal of Lithic Studies study of basalt quern quarries at Barranco Cardones and Cebolla (Gran Canaria) documents pre-European quarrying and manufacture using stone tools, despite the absence of metal tools in that context. The study identifies pecking/regularization and circular extraction negatives on quarry faces. This is important evidence that basalt could be quarried directly from bedrock using carefully staged percussion and trenching; it is not evidence that all basalt deposits or monumental block geometries were extracted identically.

### 6.3 Fire-setting: established mining technique, specific quarry claims remain contextual

Heating rock to create thermal stress, sometimes followed by cooling, is a known historical mining/quarrying method in multiple regions. Its suitability depends on rock texture, water, geometry, oxygen, access and the objective (spall a surface, create a trench, or detach a block).

Do not assume that fire-setting alone explains the Aswan Unfinished Obelisk. The specific interpretation of its tool marks and trench morphology is contested. A new 2026 Heritage Science paper proposes a more unusual molten-sodium-carbonate/natron treatment in combination with heating. It reports a small laboratory demonstration: molten sodium carbonate was applied to a pretreated granite specimen; after cooling, fine cracks were observed and the specimen fragmented after subsequent light impacts. The paper interprets a Wadi Hammamat inscription and tomb imagery as possible contextual support.

That is an interesting hypothesis, **not settled archaeological consensus**:
- No sodium-carbonate/sodium-silicate quarry residue has been identified at the relevant quarry in the paper's discussion.
- The experiment is a small specimen demonstration, not a reconstruction of a full obelisk trench or a measured ancient production process.
- The interpretation of the inscription and the relation between Wadi Hammamat greywacke/sandstone evidence and Aswan granite requires independent Egyptological and archaeometric review.
- The proposed heat/material process requires an independently replicated energy, temperature and mass balance before it can be treated as an established historical technique.

We should investigate it rather than dismiss it, but label it as a hypothesis and invite independent replication.

### 6.4 Ancient techniques as a modern experimental programme

Reconstruct four processes separately:
1. Dolerite/doleritic maul percussion and trenching.
2. Abrasive saw/core drilling with a carrier tool and loose abrasive.
3. Fire-setting / thermal shock with measured heat input, rock temperature and fragmentation.
4. The 2026 natron hypothesis as an archaeometric replication question—not a proposed modern excavation method.

Use 3D photogrammetry, structured-light scanning or CT to quantify removed volume, crack patterns, groove shape, tool wear, impact rate, abrasive composition/residue, labour, process time and uncertainty. Compare only the same geometry and task. The Amarna corundum residue should be replicated as its own drill/abrasive case; it must not be transferred automatically to Aswan granite. Preserve the archaeological evidence class and do not use reconstruction experiments to make unsupported claims about the method used at a specific monument.

## 8. Frozen experimental programme

### E0 — literature and mechanism audit

For each published result record:
- DOI and exact version/date;
- method mechanism (not only marketing name);
- rock provenance, mineralogy, grain texture, moisture and stress state;
- apparatus/geometry and operating regime;
- energy boundary and power meter location;
- gross and net removed-volume measurement method;
- fragment-size distribution and extraction method;
- test repetitions, uncertainty and independent validation status;
- whether result is analytical, synthetic, laboratory, pilot or production.

Do not manufacture row-level datasets from published averages. Where raw data are unavailable, retain the reported aggregate as a literature reference with its original denominator.

### E1 — matched specimen testbed

Use characterized, traceable granite and basalt cohorts. Randomize matched specimens across:
- mechanical-only reference;
- optimized mechanical reference;
- one assist at a time;
- candidate hybrid combinations;
- sham/negative control (same handling, no active assist);
- untreated control.

Use specimen IDs blind to the operator where practical. Freeze primary outcomes, exclusion rules and repeat-count rationale before confirmatory testing.

### E2 — instrumentation

Where applicable, capture:
- tool force, torque, speed, feed, impact/vibration and contact state;
- wall-clock and process time, including setup, cooling, flushing and cleaning;
- electrical input at the facility boundary and delivered power at the subsystem;
- fuel/chemical/cryogen/water/abrasive inputs and conversion losses;
- rock temperature field, heat-affected depth and cooling load;
- acoustic emission as a measured diagnostic stream, with source location/uncertainty separated from simulated AE;
- removed solid volume and mass, loose-spoil volume, fragment-size distribution and fines;
- tool/electrode/nozzle/transducer wear and replacements;
- maintenance, stoppages, jams, ventilation, dust and extraction burden.

The same sensor or model that controls/informs a process is not automatically an independent referee.

### E3 — metrics, no single vanity score

**Primary full-cycle outputs**
- accepted net excavated solid volume per total operating hour;
- accepted volume per primary energy input (kWh/m³), including conversion, cooling, flushing and extraction auxiliaries;
- full-cycle specific energy and its uncertainty;
- cost per accepted volume at a declared currency/date/cost boundary.

**Physical response**
- penetration/advance rate;
- normal and tangential force, torque and impact energy;
- crack/damage-zone geometry and depth;
- fragment-size distribution, overbreak, fines and usable block yield;
- wall/ground damage and support demand;
- heat-affected volume and thermal recovery.

**Reliability and maintainability**
- tool/electrode/nozzle/transducer life per accepted volume;
- failures, jams, stoppages and recovery time;
- calibration drift, serviceability and sustained duty-cycle capability.

**Environmental and human/worksite burden**
- water, fuel, cryogen, abrasive and chemical consumption;
- dust, noise, vibration, heat, emissions and waste streams;
- ventilation/cooling demand and worker exclusion burden.

**Transfer and uncertainty**
- performance by mineralogy, grain size, porosity, saturation and confining stress;
- leave-one-rock-cohort-out and leave-one-rig/configuration-out performance;
- predictive interval coverage/width, severe failure recall and out-of-domain rejection.

Do not compare drilling rate with tunnel advance rate, cutting-only time with full-cycle time, or tool-interface energy with whole-site energy.

### E4 — decision and method-selection benchmark

The method portfolio receives a declared rock profile, task geometry, available services, environmental limits and exact equipment configuration. It must:
- reject missing or unsupported capability/evidence;
- distinguish eligible, ineligible and indeterminate method/configuration pairs;
- preserve the evidence class of every material/tool/process claim;
- select no assist when the assist is not justified;
- expose trade-offs across net accepted production, energy, cost, wear, heat, water and damage;
- avoid turning a predicted fracture or simulated crack into an observed excavation result.

### E5 — pilot and transfer

Only after repeatable specimen tests:
- conduct an independently instrumented pilot in a controlled quarry, mine test area or drilling site;
- use shadow-mode analysis first;
- require second-site / second-configuration replication before transfer claims;
- maintain all existing mine/construction safety authorities and physical controller interlocks;
- never couple research software to live high-voltage, plasma, thermal, laser, cryogenic or high-pressure systems without a separately approved engineering and safety case.

## 9. Benchmark targets and comparison with SOTA

The first gate is trustworthy measurement and reproducibility, not an arbitrary performance number.

**Protocol targets**
- 100% exact replay of frozen synthetic accounting fixtures.
- 100% rejection of malformed units, non-finite values, stale identities and unsupported method/profile combinations.
- 100% of compared results disclose their energy boundary and full-cycle denominator.
- 100% of physical claims bind to source specimen, tool, configuration, calibration and independent volume measurement.
- Zero modelled/synthetic crack events counted as independent physical validation.

**Scientific comparison**
- Reproduce published claims under the same specimen/geometry and metric before comparing them.
- For hybrid methods, compare both assist-only and complete assist+mechanical system energy.
- For the 56% granite specific-cutting-energy reduction recorded in parent #1952, reproduce its exact pulse and cutting test boundaries before using it as a comparator. It is not a mine productivity or lifecycle-cost target.
- For ultrasonic assistance, first demonstrate the effect on characterized granite and basalt; the 2025 paper's reported 15.5% sandstone and 9.9% limestone speed improvements are not hard-rock granite results.
- For gyrotron drilling, replicate a declared borehole geometry and measured energy/volume boundary; do not compare a narrow hole with tunnel face advance.
- For waterjet-assisted cutting, report cooling and auxiliary energy as well as cutter loads.
- For cryogenic methods, include cryogen production, transport, storage, boil-off and heat exchange; a lower UCS is not a production gain.
- For ancient reconstruction, report removed volume and labour/energy under controlled experiments without asserting that the tested reconstruction was the unique historic method.

**Prospective performance hypothesis:** on at least one named rock/geometry regime, a hybrid method should demonstrate a statistically supported improvement in *full-cycle net accepted output per total energy or total cost* against the strongest matched mechanical baseline, while meeting wall-damage, fragment-quality, environmental and reliability constraints. Set the numerical improvement threshold only after a pilot variance estimate and a preregistered experiment design.
