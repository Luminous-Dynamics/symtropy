# Free-Flow Modular Fractal Structures (FFMFS)
## Research programme for architecture, engineering, manufacturing and space infrastructure

**Date:** 2026-11-16  
**Status:** research programme / design hypothesis  
**Claim boundary:** This programme does not establish structural strength, safety, spaceflight qualification, or superior material efficiency. "Fractal" describes a proposed multiscale design grammar; it must be measured rather than inferred from appearance.

## Executive thesis

Explore a design family in which **globally free-form, multiscale structures are assembled from a small, reusable vocabulary of modules and interfaces**.

The core proposition is not "make buildings fractal." It is:

> Keep load paths, service interfaces, assembly operations and inspection rules legible and repeatable, while allowing the geometry, spatial experience and expressive infill to vary continuously across scales.

Working name: **Free-Flow Modular Fractal Structures (FFMFS)**.

This is related to hierarchical/graded architected lattices, modular mechanical metamaterials, deployable trusses, topology optimization, triply periodic minimal surfaces (TPMS), and robotically assembled voxel/truss systems. It is not a new established material class yet.

## Why this is timely

Recent work gives real foundations:

- The 2025 Nature Materials perspective on 3D architected materials argues that the field needs better integration across length scales and timescales, beyond small periodic specimens.
- The 2025 metal-lattice additive-manufacturing review identifies process/microstructure/mechanical-property coupling, defect control and fatigue as key barriers.
- The February 2026 generative-design review describes process-aware, human-in-the-loop exploration of large lattice design spaces, combining physics-based methods and data-driven acceleration.
- Research on modular assembled composite lattices reports meter-scale prototypes and argues that assembly can overcome the size limits of monolithic printing.
- A September 2026 review of modular discrete mechanical metamaterials emphasizes that inter-module interactions govern global mechanical behavior.
- NASA's ARMADAS programme is developing builder robots and reusable voxels for autonomously assembled functional structures, including habitat structures and antenna arrays.
- NASA's in-space truss technology localizes shape-specific variation in connectors while retaining common truss modules, to support robotic assembly, servicing and modular evolution.

These are precedents and adjacent results, not proof that our proposed grammar will outperform them.

## Design principles

### 1. Free-form globally, standardized locally

A module should have a well-defined interface contract: load-transfer surfaces, fastener/lock geometry, alignment features, electrical/data/fluid service connectors where applicable, tolerance envelope, inspection access and replaceability.

The global shape may be curved, branching, nested or irregular. Avoid making every member or connector unique. A promising approach is common node/hub families plus parameterized members and a small number of orientation adapters.

### 2. Separate structure, services, envelope and expression

Do not make one fractal network perform every function.

Use four explicit layers:

1. **Primary load-bearing structure:** qualified trusses, frames, shells, beams or pressure vessels.
2. **Secondary multiscale structure:** graded lattices, branching braces, equipment racks, service supports and non-primary partitions.
3. **Environmental and service layers:** pressure boundary, thermal control, radiation/impact shielding, electrical, data, fluid, ventilation and fire/smoke systems.
4. **Expressive and user-authored infill:** panels, lighting, acoustic treatments, color, artwork, furniture, gardens, privacy screens and spatial partitions.

A user may change expressive infill without silently changing the certified load path or blocking emergency routes.

### 3. Use recursion only when it earns a function

Candidate roles for multiscale or fractal-like topology:

- Branching trusses and distributed support.
- Stiffness-to-mass optimization under spatially varying loads.
- Energy absorption and damage containment.
- Acoustic diffusion/absorption and light modulation.
- Heat-exchanger and fluid-routing surfaces, subject to pressure-drop, cleaning and fouling constraints.
- Cable, sensor and utility routing with isolation and access.
- Equipment racks and habitat partitions.
- Deployable booms, antenna supports, radiators and robotic assembly frames.

A recursive tree is not automatically a geometric fractal. A porous/complex shape is not automatically strong, light or efficient. Demonstrate scale invariance only over a declared scale range, and measure it.

### 4. Design for disassembly, repair and evolution

The system should be designed around the lifecycle:

- inspect a module;
- isolate its services;
- unload it safely;
- unlock and remove it;
- replace it with an equivalent or improved module;
- re-verify local and global behavior;
- preserve the as-built digital record.

For space use, replacement and robotic assembly are especially valuable because repair resources and astronaut time are scarce.

### 5. Co-design with occupants, not merely decorate for them

"All types of expression" should mean that inhabitants and communities can author the spaces they use without being forced into one aesthetic or one social arrangement.

Provide:
- quiet and active zones;
- private, communal and intermediate spaces;
- adjustable lighting, color, acoustic and visual complexity;
- alternative routes and multiple kinds of orientation cues;
- accessible reach, transfer, clearance and task envelopes;
- modular art, displays, cultural objects, textures and personal enclosures;
- user-controlled privacy and environmental settings;
- a stable, accessible base building with replaceable infill.

Use participatory co-design throughout requirements, prototypes, mockups and evaluation. Accessibility is not satisfied by a single minimum-width corridor or a late-stage review.

## Space-station and habitat applications

### Keep the critical boundary conservative

A space habitat has distinct, safety-critical functions: pressure containment, micrometeoroid protection, radiation protection, thermal control, fire safety, life support, structural load paths, crew access and emergency response.

Do not start by replacing a qualified pressure vessel with an unqualified fractal lattice.

Early FFMFS applications should be secondary or separately qualified structures:

- external service trusses and equipment platforms;
- robotic assembly frames and standardized attachment grids;
- deployable antenna, solar-array and radiator support structures;
- modular cargo and equipment racks;
- internal secondary frames, partitions and reconfigurable work areas;
- replaceable impact-shielding support panels;
- non-primary acoustic, lighting and privacy layers;
- cable and fluid service routing with explicit isolation and maintainability.

The primary pressure boundary, critical connections and life-support systems remain independently designed and qualified.

### A habitat as a layered architecture

**Layer A — pressure and environmental boundary:** qualified pressure shell, seals, windows, hatches and penetrations.

**Layer B — primary structure:** launch/landing/operational load paths, interfaces and local reinforcements.

**Layer C — protection:** micrometeoroid/orbital-debris shielding, radiation shielding, thermal blankets and fire/smoke barriers. Shielding depends on material, geometry and mission environment; a porous lattice is not a substitute for areal-density analysis.

**Layer D — utility spine:** power, data, thermal loops, water, air, waste and sensing, with isolation, leak detection, access and serviceability.

**Layer E — reconfigurable spatial system:** modular storage, workstations, handholds/footholds, privacy, exercise, sleep, research and communal spaces.

**Layer F — expressive infill:** occupant-selected colors, patterns, lighting, art, tactile surfaces, acoustic panels, plants/bioregenerative experiments where mission-qualified, and cultural objects.

The fractal/modular grammar is most promising in layers B, D, E and F, with much stricter qualification as it approaches A and C.

### Human factors in microgravity

Do not simply copy terrestrial floor-plan assumptions. Crew need clear orientation cues, handholds, restraint points, translation paths, emergency egress, stowage discipline, reach envelopes, task lighting, acoustic control, privacy and ways to manage visual/sensory load. A beautiful branching space that creates confusing movement, snag points, blocked egress or hard-to-reach maintenance is not good habitat design.

NASA-STD-3001 Volume 2 is a key human-systems integration and habitability reference. NASA's human-factors design studio also uses CAD, VR, physical mockups and crewed human-in-the-loop evaluation; that is the right kind of design maturity path.

## Candidate geometry and material families

Do not prescribe one fractal topology. Build a catalog with explicit roles and trade-offs:

- **Tetrahedral / triangulated trusses:** clear axial load paths, repeatable struts and nodes; connection eccentricity and buckling must be tested.
- **Hierarchical branching frames:** conformal support and distributed geometry; branch junctions and short-member congestion need scrutiny.
- **TPMS / gyroid / Schwarz surfaces:** continuous surfaces for graded cellular panels, thermal/acoustic or energy-absorption experiments; powder removal, minimum wall thickness, inspection and fatigue matter.
- **Plate- and strut-based lattices:** compare relative density, stiffness, buckling, fatigue and manufacturing quality.
- **Auxetic and re-entrant cells:** useful candidates for controlled deformation and energy absorption; do not assume negative Poisson's ratio alone improves a real component.
- **Origami / deployable modules:** high packing/deployment potential; hinge fatigue, locking, tolerance accumulation and fail-safe deployment dominate.
- **Hybrid / multi-material structures:** combine stiff load paths with compliant, damping, shielding or thermal layers; material interfaces and repairability are difficult.

Candidate material routes include metal AM, welded/bolted metal modules, composite pultrusions, bonded composites, polymer AM for non-critical interiors, inflatable membranes, and in-situ materials. They are not interchangeable: choose material and process per load, environment, repair strategy, inspection method and qualification basis.

## Paradigm shifts to investigate

1. **From shape optimization to grammar optimization:** co-optimize the module vocabulary, interface families and global topology, not just the final mesh.
2. **From monolithic efficiency to lifecycle efficiency:** include launch/transport volume, assembly, inspection, disassembly, repair, reconfiguration and reuse.
3. **From one optimal structure to robust Pareto families:** expose alternatives across mass, stiffness, energy absorption, cost, aesthetics, access, manufacturing and repair.
4. **From one scale to co-designed scales:** cell, panel, module, bay, habitat and facility should exchange constraints and measurable properties.
5. **From decoration after engineering to occupant-authored infill:** protect certified support and service systems while opening an expressive design space.
6. **From CAD nominal geometry to as-built evidence:** attach manufacturing process, material batch, inspection results, geometric metrology and repair history to each module.
7. **From fixed assembly to robotic construction and servicing:** make modules discoverable, graspable, alignable, lockable, inspectable and replaceable by robots.
8. **From single-load design to damage-tolerant networks:** test local member loss, joint degradation, impact, thermal cycles, vibration and cascading failures.
9. **From a single objective to human–structure co-design:** accessibility, sensory autonomy, dignity, cultural expression and occupant control are first-class design variables, not decoration.
10. **From visual fractality to measurable multiscale structure:** report scale-dependent metrics and only call a structure fractal when a statistically supported scaling regime exists.

## Benchmark programme

### Structural performance

- mass and material volume;
- stiffness-to-mass and stiffness by direction;
- buckling and imperfection sensitivity;
- stress/strain concentration at joints and branch junctions;
- natural frequencies, mode separation and damping;
- fatigue life under mission load spectra;
- fracture/damage tolerance and local-loss propagation;
- load-path redundancy, robustness and residual capacity;
- thermal deformation and coupled thermo-mechanical behavior.

Use finite-element analysis with mesh/convergence checks, independent solver comparisons and physical coupons. Graph cycle count, node degree or geometric appearance must never be used as a substitute for stress analysis.

### Manufacturing and assembly

- number of unique part geometries and connector families;
- assembly operations, time, robot/tool changes and alignment tolerance;
- mass/volume/packing efficiency;
- material yield, build time and energy per accepted part;
- defect rate, dimensional capability and nondestructive-inspection coverage;
- powder/support removal and inaccessible internal volume;
- connector interchangeability and repair time;
- disassembly damage, reuse rate and module replacement time;
- traceability from feedstock/batch/process to as-built part.

### Space-specific qualification

- launch mass and stowed volume;
- deployment and assembly reliability;
- vibration, shock and acceleration loads;
- thermal-vacuum and thermal-cycle response;
- outgassing/flammability as applicable;
- radiation shielding by material and areal density;
- micrometeoroid/orbital-debris performance of the qualified shielding system;
- robotic inspection and replacement;
- crew ingress/egress, translation routes and emergency operations;
- human-in-the-loop habitat mockups.

Do not convert a structural simulation into flight qualification. Additive-manufactured space hardware must meet applicable strength, fatigue, fracture-control, inspection and acceptance requirements. NASA-HDBK-5026 is an active NASA handbook providing guidance in these areas and references NASA-STD-5001, NASA-STD-5009 and NASA-STD-5019.

### Architecture and human experience

- accessible route coverage and reach/transfer envelopes;
- task completion time and error rates;
- wayfinding and orientation success;
- acoustic privacy and sound levels;
- controllability of lighting, visual complexity and sensory conditions;
- privacy choice, social choice and spatial flexibility;
- user-rated agency, belonging and comfort;
- participation quality and influence in co-design;
- cost and effort to change a space after occupancy.

Use paid and accessible participatory design with diverse occupants, not only designer preference panels.

## Baselines and comparison protocol

Compare each candidate against suitable baselines under matched constraints:

- conventional beam/truss frame;
- standard spaceframe and modular connector system;
- honeycomb sandwich panel;
- conventional strut lattice;
- TPMS/gyroid and graded-lattice alternatives;
- topology-optimized monolithic component;
- modular assembled composite lattice;
- NASA ARMADAS-like voxelized assembly concept where publicly reproducible;
- simple non-fractal branching or stochastic network.

Freeze geometry envelope, material/process, mass budget, load/boundary conditions, safety factors, manufacturing capability, solver fidelity and test protocol. Report Pareto fronts and uncertainty. A fractal design wins only if it produces a relevant improvement that survives manufacturing, connection, inspection and lifecycle costs.

## Execution roadmap

### F0 — geometry grammar and evidence contract
Create a bounded, CPU-only generator for a small branching/trussed module family. Output explicit nodes, members, connector families and deterministic configuration identity. Validate graph connectivity, duplicate edges, finite coordinates, length envelopes and topology counts. This is geometry/topology verification only—not structural analysis.

### F1 — module interface grammar
Define hub families, member end fittings, tolerances, inspection zones, fastener/locking concepts, service ports, robot grasp features and disassembly sequence. Explore common connectors with parameterized/adjustable member lengths.

### F2 — reference structural models
Integrate a beam/truss finite-element evaluator with independent analytical cases. Add axial, bending, buckling, modal and fatigue models only with clear assumptions and verification. Compare with established solvers.

### F3 — process-aware multiobjective generation
Optimize mass, stiffness, damage tolerance, buildability, assembly effort, inspection, repair and human factors together. Include uncertain material/process properties and geometric imperfections.

### F4 — prototype and metrology
Print/machine/weld a family of coupons and node/member assemblies. Scan as-built geometry, compare to nominal, test joints and members, inspect defects, and correlate model predictions with physical data.

### F5 — robotic assembly and servicing
Use a small planar/3D demonstrator and simulated/physical robot to test grasp, alignment, joining, inspection, removal and replacement. Extend existing robotics interoperability and assurance work rather than creating a parallel robot control stack.

### F6 — habitat / built-environment mockup
Build a non-pressurized full-scale interior mockup. Evaluate routes, access, task performance, acoustic/sensory controls, privacy, expression and crew/occupant agency.

### F7 — flight-relevant qualification path
Only after lower-level evidence: mission-specific structural design, materials/process qualification, nondestructive evaluation, vibration/shock, thermal vacuum, radiation/impact shielding tests and formal program acceptance.

## First executable proof

The first proof is intentionally modest:

- a recursive 3D branch/truss generator;
- stable module/member IDs;
- bounded depth/node count and parameter validation;
- graph connectivity and cycle-rank checks;
- member-length and bounding-box metrics;
- deterministic same-build replay;
- clear output stating "geometry/topology only — no strength or flight claim".

The second proof should add an independently checked truss/beam analysis and simple physical coupon, not a photorealistic render.

## Decision

Proceed with a small, measurable geometry grammar and module-interface research track. Prioritize secondary and reconfigurable structures before occupied primary structures. Treat occupant expression and sensory/accessibility choice as functional requirements. Keep structural integrity, environmental protection and life-safety systems independently qualified.



## Engineering increment — adaptive connector budgets and damage tolerance (2026-11-17)

### 1. Connector capacity is now a generative constraint

The face-attached tetrahedral generator now treats max_ports_per_node as a hard constraint during growth. Before committing a child cell, it checks all three shared face vertices. If any would exceed the interface port budget, that attachment is rejected and counted as a blocked face.

This replaces the earlier post-hoc-only check. The generator can return fewer cells than the full binary recursion upper bound; that is intentional. A smaller design that respects the connector family is preferable to an impossible hub.

Acceptance:
- zero nodes exceed the configured port budget;
- all blocked faces are counted;
- generated graph remains connected;
- each accepted child retains a valid three-point parent-face attachment;
- cell/member/node count relationships are checked against the adaptive grammar;
- deterministic same-build digest includes the blocked-face count.

### 2. Tolerance analysis is a separate engineering gate

The connector-fit module computes the worst-case radial clearance envelope from pin and bore dimensional bands. It rejects interference/insufficient clearance and excessive play. A linear stack reports both arithmetic worst-case sum and root-sum-square (RSS) estimate. RSS is not a guaranteed bound; it assumes independent centered contributions and must not replace worst-case tolerance analysis where safety or interchangeability requires it.

Next improvement: add datum schemes, positional/angular tolerances, thermal expansion, wear, coating and assembly-force models, then validate the predicted fit with physical metrology and repeated assembly cycles.

### 3. Kinematic solvability is not damage tolerance

The companion research/truss-fem-lab is a reference linear-elastic pin-jointed truss solver. The new damage module runs a frozen baseline load case and removes each member in turn. Each removal is classified as:
- still solvable, with a response envelope;
- a singular-stiffness / kinematic-mechanism outcome;
- another typed analysis failure.

Its single-removal solvability fraction is a deterministic scenario fraction, not a probability of physical survival. It must be paired with load spectra, material/joint uncertainty, failure distributions, fatigue, fracture, inspection intervals and physical tests before reliability claims.

### 4. New benchmark dimensions

The structural programme now needs to evaluate:

| Dimension | Measure | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Interface feasibility | port count, angular separation, pin/bore clearance, assembly force | avoids connector congestion and impossible fit |
| Kinematic robustness | full rank / singularity under supports and member knockouts | distinguishes connected geometry from stable frameworks |
| Damage tolerance | single-member removal envelope and multi-member cut-set response | reveals cascading loss of load path |
| Repairability | isolate, unload, remove, replace and retest time | measures lifecycle maintainability |
| Manufacturing variation | tolerance-stack failure rate and as-built deviation | bridges nominal CAD and real production |
| Robotic assembly | grasp/align/insert/lock/verify success and tool changes | measures actual autonomous buildability |
| Occupant agency | accessible options for privacy, sensory control and expressive infill | evaluates space as lived environment, not just structure |

The next damage step after single-member removal should be a bounded search over two-member combinations, ranked by load-path criticality, with a fixed scenario budget and no survivor-only reporting.

### 5. Revised design objective

Do not maximize a single graph complexity, cycle count, or stiffness-to-mass score. Evaluate a constrained Pareto family across:

- structural response and reserve capacity;
- connector standardization and tolerance feasibility;
- manufacturing yield and inspection access;
- assembly, disassembly, repair and reuse effort;
- launch/stowed volume where space-bound;
- utility routing and isolation;
- accessibility, privacy, sensory controllability and occupant-authored expression.

An FFMFS candidate only wins when it improves a preregistered, practically meaningful objective against conventional trusses/spaceframes, topology optimization, graded lattices, TPMS and modular voxel assemblies under matched materials, loads, process constraints and evaluation budget.



### 6. Bounded two-member cut-set screening

The truss reference lab now includes a second damage campaign. It selects evenly spaced ranks from the complete unordered member-pair space, with a hard 1,024-scenario execution cap. The receipt reports:
- total possible member pairs;
- requested and applied scenario budget;
- every omitted scenario count;
- stable responses, singular mechanisms and other failures;
- original member indices for each tested pair.

This is a deterministic screening sample, not exhaustive analysis and not a probability estimate. The next stage should prioritize physically credible failures from connector topology, load path, manufacturing defects and inspection data, and then use a validated progressive-damage model. No safety-critical reliability claim follows from the current linear-elastic model.

## Stability increment — member yield and elastic Euler buckling screen (2026-11-17)

The standalone truss reference lab now has research/truss-fem-lab/src/stability.rs.

For each member, the caller supplies:
- Young's modulus E;
- cross-sectional area A;
- minimum principal second moment of area I about the weak axis;
- yield strength;
- effective-length factor K.

The screen reports axial yield utilization, radius of gyration, slenderness KL/r, ideal Euler critical load, and compression-only Euler utilization when the ideal Euler formula is in its nominal elastic range. It distinguishes ElasticEulerApplicable from YieldOrInelasticRegime; short columns are not assigned a misleading elastic-Euler utilization.

### Analytical fixture

The new test uses a 2 m pin-ended axial member with E=200 GPa, I=1e-8 m^4, A=0.01 m^2, K=1, and 1 kN compression. It checks the closed-form Euler critical load and utilization. Other tests verify tension handling, the yield/inelastic slenderness regime and fail-closed property validation.

### Boundaries

This remains an ideal member-level screen. It does not solve:
- global frame or system buckling;
- beam-column bending interaction or lateral-torsional buckling;
- joint restraint, flexibility, clearance or preload;
- geometric imperfections, residual stress or plasticity;
- local plate/section buckling;
- fatigue, fracture, contact or dynamic loads;
- code-prescribed interaction equations, factors of safety or flight qualification.

The effective-length factor K and section data must be justified for any real application. A passing member screen is not structural substantiation.

### Why this is the next gate

An axial truss analysis can return finite member forces while ignoring member bending stiffness and buckling capacity. The added screen exposes one class of compression instability before the programme advances to beam-column elements and global eigenvalue buckling. The next numerical milestone should be a separate beam/frame element model, verified against analytical columns and an independent solver, with joint rotational stiffness and initial-imperfection studies.

### Expanded paradigm-shift hypothesis: interface-first, lifecycle-qualified structures

Current research on modular discrete mechanical metamaterials emphasizes that inter-module contact and constraints govern the global response, not merely the behavior of an isolated cell (Zhu et al., 2026, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijmecsci.2026.111915). The design unit should therefore be the complete interface-bearing module plus assembly context.

The FFMFE hypothesis should be tested through five coupled design loops:

1. Geometry loop: generate candidate cell/module/bay/system morphology.
2. Interface loop: enforce connector family, port count, angular access, datum scheme, tolerances and service isolation.
3. Mechanics loop: check kinematic stability, member and global buckling, stresses, fatigue and damage propagation.
4. Lifecycle loop: optimize assembly, inspection, disassembly, repair, reuse and reconfiguration.
5. Human loop: preserve accessibility, egress, privacy, sensory control, cultural expression and occupant authorship.

Each loop supplies hard constraints and uncertainty to the others. This is different from generating an attractive geometry and applying manufacturability or accessibility checks at the end.

### Revised SOTA strategy

Do not attempt to out-feature nTop, Fusion, Altair or Materialise at general-purpose CAD, lattice generation or topology optimization. They already provide powerful geometry, optimization and analysis workflows. Instead, use them as reference evaluators and compete on an integrated, open, evidence-bearing structural grammar:

- portable typed module and connector contracts;
- deterministic assembly and service plans;
- fit and tolerance closure before fabrication;
- mechanical verification at member, joint and system scales;
- inspectability and repair as optimization dimensions;
- as-built metrology and process traceability;
- occupant-authored spatial infill separated from qualified structure.

A meaningful result requires an independently verified improvement on at least one preregistered lifecycle metric without unacceptable loss elsewhere in the Pareto set.



## Interface-compliance and mass-normalized comparison increment — 2026-11-17

### Joint compliance is now explicit in the reference model

The new research/truss-fem-lab/src/interfaces.rs provides an alternate truss solve with one axial translational spring at each member end. The member and the two end springs act in series:

\[
C_\mathrm{axial} = \frac{L}{EA} + \frac{1}{k_\mathrm{from}} + \frac{1}{k_\mathrm{to}},
\qquad
k_\mathrm{effective} = \frac{1}{C_\mathrm{axial}}.
\]

The result separates:
- total node-to-node extension;
- actual bar extension and axial stress;
- connector slip at each member end;
- energy stored in the bar and in the two connector springs.

An analytical 2 m bar test uses 1 GN/m springs at both ends. Under 1 kN tension, bar extension remains 1 micrometre while each connector slips 1 micrometre, giving 3 micrometres total. A very stiff-spring test approaches the original rigid-joint solver. This validates the series-compliance implementation; it does not calibrate a real connector.

### Interface model boundaries

This is a one-dimensional axial spring idealization. It does not model rotational stiffness, fastener preload, friction, bearing/contact, clearance take-up, backlash, bolt slip, nonlinear behavior, local stress, multiaxial interaction or joint fatigue. It is the next rung above a perfect pin joint, not a realistic bolt or self-locking connector model.

Next steps:
1. Add six-degree-of-freedom connector stiffness matrices (three translations and three rotations).
2. Add preload, clearance/gap and contact state with a nonlinear incremental solver.
3. Calibrate connector models from physical coupons, measured load-displacement curves and repeated assembly cycles.
4. Include temperature, wear and manufacturing tolerance distributions.
5. Validate with independent commercial/academic FE models and physical assemblies.

### Mass-normalized candidate evaluation

The research/truss-fem-lab/src/benchmark.rs module now derives a uniform solid-round member cross-section from a declared member-only mass budget and material density. It then executes the candidate under the supplied boundary/load case and reports:
- nodes, members and total member length;
- target and computed member mass;
- derived cross-sectional area and weak-axis second moment;
- peak displacement, stress, strain energy and equilibrium residual;
- yield utilization and ideal Euler utilization;
- single-member removal solvability;
- bounded two-member removal results and omitted-pair counts.

For a solid circular member, the idealized weak-axis second moment is computed from A as I=A^2/(4*pi). This is a declared section hypothesis, not a universal section model.

**Critical comparability condition:** the caller must freeze the same external envelope, support coordinates, load cases, material constitutive assumptions, density, member-mass accounting boundary, failure scenarios, numerical tolerances and search budget across all candidate topologies. The present mass budget includes only straight members. It excludes hubs, connectors, fasteners, service routing, stowage, manufacturing waste, inspection, robotic assembly, maintenance and lifecycle effects. Therefore it is not a whole-system mass or cost victory.

### Revised comparison ladder

| Level | Candidate comparison | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| L0 | Geometry/topology grammar | deterministic output, valid geometry, bounded complexity |
| L1 | Perfect-joint axial truss | analytical member tests, support stability, equilibrium residual |
| L2 | Joint-compliant axial truss | calibrated spring stiffness and separate bar/joint deformation |
| L3 | Member yield and Euler screen | section properties, K-factor justification, correct slenderness regime |
| L4 | Beam/frame and global stability | verified beam elements, eigenvalue buckling, imperfections and joint rotational stiffness |
| L5 | Manufacturing and connector coupons | metrology, tolerance closure, load-displacement, repeated fit/assembly |
| L6 | System lifecycle | mass/cost/repair/inspection/robot assembly and as-built evidence |
| L7 | Habitat/flight qualification | mission-specific standards, safety authority, environmental testing and formal acceptance |

No level should inherit a qualification claim from the previous one. Each higher level adds new physics and new evidence obligations.

### First matched topology fixture

The benchmark harness now generates two small, deterministic reference candidates with:
- the same regular-tetrahedron external envelope;
- the same external support coordinates and nodal load;
- the same material density, modulus and yield-strength assumptions;
- the same member-only mass budget, with cross-sectional area derived separately for each candidate.

Candidate A is a regular tetrahedral frame with six outer members. Candidate B retains the same external frame and adds an interior hub connected to all four outer vertices. Both are evaluated with the same axial truss, member-yield/Euler and damage-scenario routines.

This test is intentionally small. It isolates the effect of added internal connectivity under a matched member-mass budget. It does not include connector/hub mass, rotational stiffness, assembly effort or manufacturing yield; therefore it is not a system-level architecture comparison. The result is allowed to show that the added hub is worse.

### Compute-aware damage-screen budgets

The bounded two-member removal campaign now uses both a hard 1,024-scenario ceiling and an approximate dense-solve work budget proportional to the cube of the number of free degrees of freedom. The applied sample is bounded by the request, available pairs, the hard scenario ceiling and the work budget. Every receipt reports requested, applied and omitted scenarios. This is an execution-budget guard, not a reliability metric; sampled pair ranks remain deterministic and are not a probability distribution.
