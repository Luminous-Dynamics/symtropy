# Ground-Level Diegetic Artifacts and Ordinary Voices Anthology

## Purpose

Design bibles explain a society. People usually encounter it through a receipt, a broken sign, a meal, a rumor, a badly written message, or a document whose author assumed everyone already knew the context.

This anthology provides examples of low-prestige evidence. These artifacts are not neutral lore delivery. They are partial, biased, funny, wrong, intimate, bureaucratic, or disposable.

> **The world should sometimes reveal more through what people throw away than through what institutions preserve.**

## Artifact rules

1. An artifact must have an author, audience, and practical reason to exist.
2. It may be inaccurate without becoming random.
3. It should expose vocabulary, assumptions, and material conditions.
4. It should not explain every reference.
5. Valuable evidence may coexist with boredom, advertising, or family conflict.
6. The player should sometimes misunderstand an artifact until later context arrives.

## Authoring guidance

For every flagship campaign packet, include at least six artifacts with different social positions:

```text
institutional
household
worker
child or learner
commercial or entertainment
hostile, joking, or dismissive
```

At least one artifact should be boring. At least one should be wrong. At least one should reveal affection or irritation unrelated to the campaign's central issue.

Artifacts should not all be collectible text panels. They may appear through:

- object wear;
- signage;
- audio fragments;
- food labels;
- uniforms;
- interface defaults;
- gestures;
- songs;
- pricing;
- repair modifications;
- local jokes repeated in dialogue.

## Closing principle

> **The lore is deepest when people use history for purposes other than teaching the player.**
