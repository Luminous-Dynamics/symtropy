# Human Irrationality, Status, Rivalry, Revenge, and Miscalculation Atlas

## Purpose

Symtropy's systems correctly model scarcity, authority, infrastructure, ecology, custody, labor, and institutional incentives. Human beings do not become rational merely because those systems are legible.

People also act because:

```text
they want to be admired
they are embarrassed
they love the wrong person
they hate being corrected
they believe a story about their ancestors
they are bored
they mistake confidence for competence
they want their district to win
they cannot forgive an insult
they fear looking weak
they envy a sibling
they are faithful
they are superstitious
they want to wear something beautiful
they enjoy the fight
```

These motives should not replace structural causes. They bend, accelerate, misread, and sometimes overwhelm them.

> **A pressure creates a field of possible decisions. A person can still choose the foolish one for a reason that matters only to them.**

## Design boundaries

- Irrational does not mean random.
- Trauma does not automatically produce violence.
- Culture does not mechanically determine personality.
- Villainy does not require secret systemic wisdom.
- Good intentions do not guarantee competence.
- A character may refuse the ethically best vocabulary available to the designers.
- Some conflicts end through exhaustion, luck, death, fashion change, or unrelated crisis rather than moral resolution.

# Motive families

## 1. Status

Status may come from wealth, lineage, competence, danger, piety, beauty, technical mastery, sacrifice, popularity, or refusal to care what others think.

Status systems create behavior that may be materially irrational but socially coherent.

Example:

A Vanta workshop refuses an efficient local brace design because accepting it would acknowledge that an uncertified migrant repaired what three charter members failed to diagnose.

The material issue is interoperability. The immediate cause of escalation is humiliation.

## 2. Revenge

Revenge is not always a utility calculation. A person may accept personal loss to ensure another person's loss is publicly recognized.

Revenge can target:

- the actual perpetrator;
- their family;
- an institution;
- a symbol;
- a district;
- a profession;
- a historical narrative;
- someone merely associated with the harm.

A justice process may establish responsibility and still fail to satisfy the person seeking revenge.

## 3. Romantic jealousy and attachment

Relationships can alter faction decisions without becoming romance mechanics.

Examples:

- a port official delays a rival's cargo inspection after learning that rival is courting their former partner;
- a council delegate supports an unsafe tower because their spouse designed it;
- a rescue captain diverts toward an old crew member while insisting the choice was purely tactical;
- two activists split a movement after a breakup and rewrite the disagreement as ideology.

The game should not reduce these to secret “jealousy meters.” They emerge from relationships, memories, and specific choices.

## 4. Family rivalry

Households contain inheritance disputes, sibling comparison, parental favoritism, generational resentment, and conflicting ideas of duty.

A public office may be shaped by a private family history nobody outside the household fully understands.

## 5. Faith and certainty

Belief can sustain care, courage, meaning, and restraint. It can also make compromise feel like betrayal.

A person may reject a technically safe solution because it violates a sacred obligation, or accept dangerous service because they believe it completes one.

Faith should not be coded as ignorance. Certainty can exist among brilliant people.

## 6. Nationalism and territorial pride

People may care about a place because it is home, because ancestors defended it, because rivals mock it, or because losing it would feel like public humiliation.

They may defend a structurally obsolete district rather than accept relocation, even when compensation is generous.

## 7. Honor and face

Military crews, rescue teams, workshops, sports clubs, families, and religious orders may preserve honor codes.

Honor can support courage and mutual obligation. It can also discourage retreat, confession, assistance, or adaptation.

## 8. Boredom and thrill

Long-lived, materially secure societies still produce boredom.

Boredom can drive:

- dangerous races;
- unauthorized exploration;
- sabotage framed as a prank;
- illegal body modification;
- extremist aesthetics;
- conspiracy communities;
- romanticized crime;
- political movements that promise intensity.

## 9. Fashion and taste

A technically superior design may lose because it looks provincial. A dangerous material may return because celebrities wear it. An old corporate interface may survive because it feels prestigious.

Taste produces infrastructure consequences.

## 10. Miscalculation

Institutions act on incomplete information. Smart people make bad forecasts. Models omit variables. Subordinates soften reports. Leaders overestimate loyalty. Opponents misunderstand one another.

Not every disaster requires corruption or Null capture.

# Authored conflict fragments

## 1. The second-place engineer

**Place:** Nine Pumps.

Jori Bale has maintained the oldest pump for twenty-six years. A younger operator, Priya Namas, develops a quieter and more efficient impeller profile.

Jori publicly argues the design cannot survive flood grit. Privately, he fears retirement and the loss of being the person everyone calls first.

His concern is not fabricated: the prototype really has insufficient grit testing. His opposition is also personal.

Possible outcomes include:

- a combined test proving both right in different ways;
- Jori sabotaging the trial and being discovered;
- Priya leaving for Aureline;
- the council adopting the design prematurely to embarrass Jori;
- a flood making the rivalry irrelevant before either can resolve it.

## 2. The councilor's ugly tower

**Place:** Aster Canyon.

Councilor Daro champions a launch-support tower that violates shade tradition. The official argument concerns employment and orbital access.

The private motive is that Daro's late father designed the original rejected version. Daro wants the skyline to prove the family was right.

The tower can still be economically useful. Exposing the motive does not settle the policy.

## 3. Dockball and the customs riot

**Place:** C-Port.

A customs delay occurs on the night Ceres plays Mars in a dockball final. Officials extend shifts with undertrained temporary staff. A disputed call in the match becomes entangled with anger over Martian cargo inspections.

The resulting riot is later taught as a trade conflict. Many participants remember it as a sports riot. Both are incomplete.

## 4. The memorial seat

**Place:** Sargasso Ring.

Two families dispute who may occupy a ceremonial council seat associated with the Air Strike. One family descends from a famous victim. The other descends from the technician who kept the district sealed afterward but was omitted from memorial histories.

The immediate conflict is status, grief, and public recognition—not resource scarcity.

## 5. The lover at the hearing

**Place:** Palinode Deep.

A witness supports one continuity claimant because that person remembers a private relationship. The witness knows memory may be reconstructed. They also desperately want the relationship to have survived.

Their testimony is sincere and biased.

## 6. The pious smuggler

**Network:** Unlicensed Passage.

A route captain refuses payment to move a persecuted household, then charges an impoverished migrant family three times the normal rate. The captain believes the first act was sacred duty and the second necessary commerce.

They do not experience the contradiction as hypocrisy.

## 7. The reactor name

A settlement spends months arguing whether a rebuilt reactor should be named after a corporate engineer, a worker collective, or nobody.

The delay causes real maintenance risk. The argument continues because the name determines whose story children will learn.

## 8. The fashionable seal

A Lucent celebrity wears an antique pressure-seal collar in a music performance. Reproductions become popular. Several are structurally decorative and interfere with emergency suits.

Kestrel bans them. The ban makes them more desirable.

## 9. The veteran's corridor

A retired fleet officer opposes converting an old defense corridor into housing because former crew died there. Housing advocates call the claim militarized nostalgia. The officer's adult child supports the conversion and believes the memorial culture consumed their family.

## 10. The mayor who cannot apologize

A mayor makes a minor technical error during a storm briefing. Correcting it immediately would have little cost. A rival mocks the mistake publicly. The mayor doubles down to avoid humiliation, and the small error becomes a dangerous policy.

## 11. The school rivalry that became jurisdiction

Two schools on opposite sides of a regional border compete in robotics. A disputed championship leads to accusations of component smuggling, then customs inspections, then a broader jurisdiction case.

Decades later historians describe the dispute as a standards conflict. Alumni still call it the stolen final.

## 12. The bored habitat heirs

Children of a stable habitat elite create an illegal exploration club. They enter sealed industrial spaces for prestige and record the trips for Lucent feeds.

One expedition uncovers evidence of corporate wrongdoing. Another damages a pressure barrier.

They are simultaneously reckless, privileged, and historically useful.

## 13. The saint's bad recipe

A beloved founder's terrible emergency ration recipe becomes ceremonial food. Everyone dislikes it. Admitting that feels disrespectful.

A young chef changes one ingredient and is accused of erasing history. The founder's surviving letters reveal she also hated the recipe.

## 14. The rescue captain's grudge

A Passage captain follows every formal rescue rule but repeatedly assigns a rival vessel the least prestigious support tasks. During a crisis, the rival becomes the only ship able to perform an intercept.

The captain must choose whether to admit dependence in front of the fleet.

## 15. The referendum romance

Two public figures from opposing secession campaigns begin a relationship. Supporters interpret every compromise as betrayal. The couple's genuine disagreements become impossible to separate from public suspicion.

Their relationship may survive while the federation does not, or vice versa.

## 16. The cheap monument

A regional government commissions an enormous memorial because elections are near. The monument is structurally poor, artistically unpopular, and built by a company connected to the minister.

Years later local skaters, vendors, and children make it beloved for uses never intended by the sponsors.

## 17. The prophet who guessed correctly

A fringe preacher predicts a corridor failure for numerological reasons. The corridor fails due to maintenance debt.

Followers treat the event as proof. Engineers struggle to communicate causality without sounding as though they are denying the visible prediction.

## 18. The expert who lies about not knowing

A respected ecologist is unsure whether a nonhuman signal indicates distress. They fear losing authority and present a confident interpretation.

The interpretation later proves partly correct for the wrong reasons.

## 19. The family that likes the company town

A worker household opposes liberation from a corporate habitat. The company provided education, predictable housing, and a social identity. The proposed successor government appears unstable and culturally alien.

Other workers call them collaborators. Their attachment is not merely false consciousness.

## 20. The war nobody intended

Two fleets perform defensive maneuvers designed to communicate resolve without attack. Each uses doctrine based on outdated assumptions about the other's sensor range. A collision destroys a civilian tender.

Political leaders reinterpret the accident as aggression because admitting miscalculation would undermine authority.

# Integrating irrational motives with simulation

An irrational motive must still enter the causal model through grounded state.

Possible fields:

```text
status_threat
public_humiliation
private_attachment
family_obligation
sacred_commitment
territorial_identity
rivalry_target
prestige_goal
boredom_pressure
risk_appetite
aesthetic_preference
revenge_commitment
confidence_error
```

These are not universal scalar meters exposed to players. They are authoring and runtime factors with origins, contexts, thresholds, and privacy.

## Proposal boundary

A motive may alter:

- attention;
- interpretation;
- willingness to compromise;
- perceived cost;
- preferred plan;
- dialogue framing;
- coalition behavior.

It may not invent authority, resources, evidence, or completed actions.

## Explanation boundary

The game should not automatically tell the player:

```text
Jori opposed the impeller because status_threat = 0.83
```

The player may infer through behavior, relationships, history, testimony, or later confession.

## Failure without lesson

Some arcs should end badly without producing a neat social reform.

A death may harden the wrong myth. A scandal may improve one institution and worsen another. A foolish fashion may simply pass. A family may never reconcile. A person may understand their mistake and still refuse to admit it.

# Authoring audit

For every major campaign, ask:

- Who wants recognition rather than resources?
- Who is embarrassed?
- Who believes compromise would dishonor someone dead?
- Who benefits socially from a technically bad decision?
- Which relationship is altering public behavior?
- Who is bored enough to create danger?
- What fashionable or aesthetic preference changes material choices?
- Which leader misunderstands the situation?
- Which conflict cannot be solved by presenting better evidence?
- Which person is simply cruel, petty, or vain without becoming a universal monster?

Not every campaign needs all of these. A campaign with none of them risks feeling designed by institutions rather than lived by people.

## Closing principle

> **People do not stop being structural creatures when they act irrationally. Their irrationality is one of the structures other people must survive.**
