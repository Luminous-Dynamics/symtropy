# Continuance Crawler Interior, Memory, and Survivor Life Atlas

## Purpose

Define how the first mobile base becomes emotionally recognizable, socially inhabited, and historically continuous from the Breaking of Firstlight through planetary travel and eventual spacefaring transformation.

The crawler must feel like a real interior whose inconveniences, repairs, customs, arguments, and objects become familiar.

# 1. The First Interior State

Immediately after departure, the crawler is not comfortable.

It is:

- overloaded;
- noisy;
- wet or dusty;
- poorly balanced;
- full of strangers and partial acquaintances;
- missing supplies;
- carrying disputed objects;
- operating under temporary authority;
- physically marked by the escape.

The interior should contain visible evidence of the final loading decisions.

If the player chose medical supplies, the medical berth is functional but a sleeping space may be lost.

If the player chose an archive core, a cargo aisle may remain obstructed.

If the player chose more survivors, people sleep in work areas and privacy becomes difficult.

# 2. Core Interior Zones

## 2.1 Cab

A working space for driving, navigation, route communication, and immediate decisions.

Memory details:

- an old route sticker;
- a cracked display repaired during the opening;
- a second seat that belonged to the previous keeper;
- improvised controls fitted to the current driver;
- a view of the Firstlight sign carried on the hood or dashboard.

Social pressure:

- who is allowed in the cab;
- whether the player may override the driver;
- whether someone sleeps there during crowding;
- who hears private route negotiations.

## 2.2 Commons

A narrow shared area for food, meetings, games, arguments, and rest.

It should not resemble a luxury lounge.

Memory details:

- folding table with mismatched repairs;
- seats that shift during rough travel;
- community notices;
- one terrible lamp preserved from Firstlight;
- stains and marks that residents name.

## 2.3 Workshop

A material center of the player's early competence.

Memory details:

- tools selected during evacuation;
- empty outlines where lost tools belonged;
- a bench built from a Firstlight door;
- a mentor's marking system;
- unresolved ownership labels;
- a repair that everyone can hear when it loosens.

## 2.4 Medical Berth

A care space requiring privacy, sanitation, power, water, and authority.

Memory details:

- a curtain made from festival fabric;
- one obsolete cabinet retained because the seals still work;
- medication lists for people no longer aboard;
- a handover recording from the Breaking;
- a contested bed used as ordinary sleeping space when empty.

## 2.5 Bunks and Personal Alcoves

Small spaces that reveal inequality and negotiation.

Memory details:

- who sleeps near heat;
- who needs quiet;
- who can climb;
- who has the only private enclosure;
- which bunk remains empty for a missing person;
- who refuses to reserve a space for someone unlikely to return.

## 2.6 Sanitation and Water

A practical site of dignity, maintenance, embarrassment, illness, and conflict.

Memory details:

- a pump with a distinctive sound;
- water-use agreements;
- adaptations for different bodies;
- the smell that indicates filter failure;
- graffiti about shower schedules;
- one resident who always cleans without recognition.

## 2.7 Cargo Deck

A changing landscape of practical and symbolic priority.

Memory details:

- a crate nobody is authorized to open;
- ecological cultures under improvised light;
- personal objects tied together for rough roads;
- disputed corporate components;
- empty space created by trade or loss;
- chalk load lines updated by different hands.

## 2.8 Roof and Exterior Walkway

A place for maintenance, weather, watch, private conversation, and wonder.

Memory details:

- handholds polished by use;
- a damaged panel from the Breaking;
- a small antenna built by an apprentice;
- a place where residents watch stars;
- route trophies and practical debris.

# 3. Firstlight Memory Objects

Each worldline should carry eight to twenty objects with different significance.

Possible categories:

## 3.1 Practical Heirloom

A tool, pump, kettle, sensor, stretcher, route key, or spare part that continues doing work.

## 3.2 Personal Object

Clothing, photograph, game piece, musical instrument, letter, body-support item, toy, or keepsake.

## 3.3 Public Object

A sign, charter copy, clinic record, route map, public seal, maintenance board, or community fund ledger.

## 3.4 Cultural Object

Recipe culture, instrument, costume, festival light, recording, artwork, joke archive, or ritual material.

## 3.5 Ecological Object

Seeds, water culture, microbial bank, animal enclosure, soil, wetland data, or restoration tools.

## 3.6 Dangerous Object

Metric fragment, contaminated sample, weapon, proprietary core, disputed evidence, or unknown alien material.

Objects should be used, moved, damaged, argued over, inherited, or transformed.

They should not remain static collectibles on a trophy shelf.

# 4. The Empty Place

The crawler may retain one absence marker:

- empty bunk;
- unused cup;
- unclaimed locker;
- seat left open;
- maintenance shift still named after a missing person;
- voice message nobody agrees to delete.

Residents may disagree over how long the marker should remain.

The game must not assume preservation is always healthier or more respectful than reuse.

# 5. First Nights Aboard

## Night One: Stabilization

People are injured, exhausted, angry, and uncertain.

Possible events:

- filter failure;
- someone cannot sleep near a machine charger;
- a resident discovers their medication was left behind;
- two households claim the same bunk;
- a child or apprentice asks whether Firstlight still exists;
- a companion refuses to discuss the dead;
- the rival contacts the crawler with a route offer;
- someone cooks an inadequate meal.

## Night Two: Authority

Emergency command begins to lose legitimacy.

Questions:

- Who chooses the route?
- Who controls public supplies?
- Who has access to the archive?
- Who may invite another survivor aboard?
- Is the crawler still municipal property?
- What work is expected?

## Night Three: Home or Vehicle

Residents begin changing the space.

- partitions appear;
- tools move;
- a song is played;
- someone paints over a public label;
- a cleaning schedule is proposed;
- the player chooses or negotiates personal space;
- one resident announces plans to leave at the next settlement.

# 6. Survivor Archetypes Aboard

The exact people are worldline-generated, but the mobile household should usually include a subset of:

- technical keeper;
- driver or navigator;
- clinician or care worker;
- public-record custodian;
- cultural worker;
- machine person;
- dependent or apprentice;
- survivor with no immediately useful specialist role;
- person injured or changed by the Breaking;
- person who did not want to leave;
- person who sees the catastrophe as opportunity;
- temporary passenger.

A survivor without an obvious gameplay function is important. People do not earn the right to evacuation by granting bonuses.

# 7. Ordinary-Life Scene Packets

## 7.1 The Rattling Drawer

Everyone knows which road vibration opens one drawer. Several people propose repairs; nobody prioritizes it until a critical object falls out.

## 7.2 The First Shared Meal

Ingredients from different households produce a meal that is culturally meaningful and objectively disappointing.

## 7.3 Shower Arbitration

Water, work schedules, disability access, privacy, and dignity collide over a small sanitation system.

## 7.4 The Missing Tool

The tool is not stolen. It was moved by someone following a different organizational system.

## 7.5 Bad Route Television

A surviving local serial becomes shared entertainment. Machine characters criticize its portrayal of artificial people.

## 7.6 Roof Weather

Residents climb onto the roof during safe travel to watch a storm, migration, launch, or unfamiliar sky.

## 7.7 The Unwanted Memorial

A resident objects to turning a useful surface into a memorial wall.

## 7.8 The Guest

A temporary passenger disrupts assumptions about privacy, risk, and belonging.

## 7.9 The First Repair That Holds

A repaired system remains quiet through a difficult route. The moment is small but satisfying.

## 7.10 The Celebration of Nothing

The crawler reaches an ordinary safe campsite. People celebrate because no emergency occurred.

# 8. Personalization

The player may shape:

- bunk or room;
- tool organization;
- lighting;
- music;
- paint and exterior marks;
- clothing storage;
- workstation;
- route map;
- memorial objects;
- trophies;
- plants;
- religious or philosophical practice;
- body-support equipment.

Shared areas require consent or procedure.

Residents may contribute their own styles, resulting in an interior that is not controlled by one aesthetic menu.

# 9. Sound Identity

The crawler should be recognizable by sound.

Reference layers:

- propulsion rhythm;
- suspension and structure;
- water pump;
- ventilation;
- workshop tools;
- loose drawer;
- residents' footsteps and bodies;
- machine charging;
- radio and music;
- weather;
- exterior contact;
- IRIS spatial voice.

Changes in sound should communicate maintenance and damage.

A player should notice that home is unhealthy before a warning panel appears.

# 10. Smell, Light, and Material

The crawler has a changing sensory profile:

- hot insulation;
- wet clothes;
- metal dust;
- antiseptic;
- food;
- battery chemistry;
- soil and plants;
- smoke;
- new paint;
- unfamiliar planetary air later in progression.

Lighting differs by:

- power state;
- shift;
- weather;
- privacy;
- medical use;
- emergency;
- cultural practice.

Materials should show repair generations.

# 11. Route Cultures

As the crawler travels, residents develop:

- departure rituals;
- route songs;
- maintenance sayings;
- seating customs;
- emergency hand signals;
- jokes about difficult terrain;
- hospitality rules;
- ways of marking safe water;
- traditions for welcoming or refusing guests.

These practices may spread to other convoys or be rejected by new residents.

# 12. Relationship to Settlements

The crawler's arrival can be perceived as:

- refugees;
- customers;
- public service;
- threat;
- labor source;
- disease risk;
- political symbol;
- salvage opportunity;
- entertainment;
- evidence carrier.

Residents may have different relationships with the same settlement.

One may seek citizenship. Another may refuse to stop. Another may have family there.

# 13. Convoy Life

When other vehicles join, new ordinary life appears:

- shared meals between vehicles;
- route gossip;
- children or apprentices moving among households;
- towing disputes;
- unequal maintenance burdens;
- romances;
- trade;
- sound and light signals;
- disagreement over pace;
- vehicles leaving.

The player's crawler should not become the royal flagship by default.

# 14. Conflict Packets

## 14.1 The Route Vote

A safer route delays medical care. A faster route risks the overloaded chassis.

## 14.2 The Public Clinic

Residents disagree whether the medical module must serve outsiders when supplies are low.

## 14.3 The Archive Lock

A custodian refuses player access to Firstlight records.

## 14.4 The Weapon Mount

Some residents believe defense is necessary. Others believe it will change how every settlement sees them.

## 14.5 The Corporate Lien

A claimant offers parts and legal passage in exchange for recognized control.

## 14.6 The Passenger Who Will Not Leave

A temporary passenger lacks a safe destination.

## 14.7 The Original Keeper Returns

Someone thought missing reaches the crawler and disputes its transformation.

## 14.8 The Crawler Becomes a Building

A community wants to immobilize the vehicle and use it as permanent infrastructure.

# 15. Long-Horizon Transformations

## Five Years

The crawler may become:

- professional expedition home;
- public relief unit;
- cooperative convoy hub;
- private luxury vehicle with contested history;
- armed carrier;
- traveling clinic;
- cultural caravan;
- workshop school;
- ecological restoration rig.

## Twenty Years

The original residents may have:

- left;
- died;
- reconstituted;
- raised children;
- founded settlements;
- created rival convoys;
- transformed modules;
- disputed ownership.

## Planetary Expansion

The crawler may travel under alien skies, requiring environmental modules and local permission.

## Spacefaring Continuity

The original cab, archive, tool bench, sign, or water culture may be installed in a spacecraft habitat.

The object should carry provenance and conflicting memories.

# 16. Return to Firstlight

When the crawler returns, possible scenes include:

- survivors recognize an exterior repair;
- an object aboard is claimed by a rebuilt institution;
- the current Firstlight government denies the crawler's emergency authority;
- a missing resident's family asks what was carried;
- the vehicle is treated as a memorial the residents still need as a home;
- a machine left behind communicates with the crawler's archive;
- a young resident has no memory of the original place;
- the player sees that the crawler preserved one version of Firstlight, not the whole truth.

# 17. Content Guardrails

Do not make the crawler:

- constantly miserable;
- perfectly cozy immediately;
- a mandatory family fantasy;
- a party-selection menu;
- infinitely expandable;
- the only meaningful home;
- immune to resident departure;
- controlled through one morale meter;
- filled only with useful specialists;
- a substitute for visiting settlements.

## Atlas Maxim

> **The first home was a place the player entered. The second home is a place the survivors must negotiate into existence while the road keeps moving beneath it.**
