# Contested Histories, Archival Schools, and Public Memory Atlas

## Principle

The player may access authoritative current game state where mechanics require it. They should not receive one omniscient interpretation of history.

Historical schools differ because they preserve different evidence, ask different questions, serve different communities, and possess different blind spots.

> **Disagreement is not proof that truth is impossible. It is a reason to preserve evidence, provenance, uncertainty, and power analysis.**

# 1. Continuity Historians

Focus on institutions that preserved services, law, and social order through collapse.

**Preferred evidence:** administrative records, infrastructure logs, treaties, succession documents.

**Strength:** explains why some systems endured.

**Blind spot:** may treat stability as legitimacy and understate people excluded by continuity institutions.

# 2. Labor Archivists

Trace maintenance, care, extraction, logistics, and the people whose work made official achievements possible.

**Preferred evidence:** shift logs, union records, oral history, injury records, tool provenance, household testimony.

**Strength:** restores erased labor and material causality.

**Blind spot:** can interpret every institution primarily through class conflict and understate ecological or technical constraints.

# 3. Corporate Restorationists

Argue that companies preserved civilization when states and commons failed.

**Preferred evidence:** survival rates, service metrics, investment records, engineering archives, client petitions.

**Strength:** takes corporate competence and public demand seriously.

**Blind spot:** often treats contractual agreement as sufficient consent and discounts dependency.

# 4. Refugee and Diaspora Oral Networks

Preserve history through households, route communities, ritual, objects, food, and testimony.

**Strength:** records lives absent from official archives and preserves uncertainty around the missing.

**Blind spot:** memory changes through retelling; internal elites may define a singular diaspora story.

# 5. Machine Witness Schools

Use long-duration sensor, custody, and procedural records produced by machine systems.

**Strength:** temporal precision, large-scale pattern recognition, preservation through human institutional collapse.

**Blind spot:** sensors record what they were designed to observe; procedural consistency is not moral understanding; private life may be overexposed.

# 6. Ecological Historians

Interpret human history through watersheds, species migration, soil, disease, atmosphere, and resource flows.

**Strength:** exposes environmental externalities and nonhuman consequence.

**Blind spot:** may flatten human agency or treat communities only as pressures on systems.

# 7. Reconstitution Historians

Study memory discontinuity, returned witnesses, source chains, and identity conflict.

**Strength:** can compare accounts separated by death and restoration.

**Blind spot:** restored memory may be incomplete, altered, or treated as more authoritative than living witnesses.

# 8. Lithic Jurists

Prefer durable physical records, carved law, mechanical evidence, and publicly inspectable structures.

**Strength:** resistance to remote tampering and archive revision.

**Blind spot:** durability can preserve injustice and privilege what was important enough to carve.

# 9. Lucent Contextualists

Use media provenance, attention pathways, public framing, and search visibility to explain how societies came to know events.

**Strength:** reveals technically truthful manipulation and narrative infrastructure.

**Blind spot:** risks interpreting reality mainly through representation rather than material life.

# 10. Null Archaeologists

Study systems that preserved obsolete assumptions, duplicated categories, or continued policy after authority disappeared.

**Strength:** detects how automated history becomes active infrastructure.

**Blind spot:** may see Null influence everywhere and discount ordinary error, conflict, or institutional inertia.

# 11. Alien and Nonhuman Historiographies

Nonhuman agents may organize history around:

```text
seasonal cycles
migration paths
chemical traces
shared memory fields
pressure and acoustic events
lineage transformations
territorial flows
extinction and restoration
```

Human political dates may be irrelevant or actively misleading to them.

# 12. Public Memory Institutions

History appears through:

- museums and memorials;
- school curricula;
- public hearings;
- ritual calendars;
- ruined infrastructure;
- family archives;
- media feeds;
- tourism;
- songs and food;
- legal precedent;
- machine witnesses;
- contested place names.

Each institution has ownership, maintenance, access, correction, and privacy politics.

# 13. Propaganda Families

### Heroic Compression

One founder, battle, or invention replaces a network of labor and circumstance.

### Neutral Administration

Violence is translated into policy language: relocation, compliance, capacity management, service adjustment.

### Catastrophe Naturalization

Political vulnerability is presented as unavoidable disaster.

### Statistical Mercy

Aggregate survival improvement is used to dismiss specific preventable harm.

### Archive Flooding

Truth is not deleted; it is buried under irrelevant, duplicated, or low-quality material.

### Ancestral Mandate

Old sacrifice or founding service is used to justify current authority.

### Purity Narrative

Cultural mixture, dissent, and internal diversity are erased to construct a unified people.

# 14. Historical Investigation Gameplay

A historical inquiry may require:

```text
physical inspection
archive provenance
witness interviews
machine logs
material conservation
site archaeology
media pathway analysis
translation uncertainty
privacy negotiation
institutional access
```

Possible outcomes include:

- strong conclusion;
- likely interpretation;
- competing supported accounts;
- evidence of forgery;
- irrecoverable uncertainty;
- truth known but ethically restricted;
- public correction;
- legal action;
- memorial reform;
- deliberate nonpublication.

The player should not always be rewarded for maximal exposure.

# 15. Correction Without Reset

When a public history changes:

- the prior narrative remains part of the record;
- institutions decide whether and how to correct;
- believers respond differently;
- reputations change by domain;
- affected families may demand privacy or restitution;
- monuments may be altered, contextualized, removed, or defended;
- political movements may appropriate the correction;
- false versions may continue circulating.

History changes society through transmission, not through an instant truth flag.
