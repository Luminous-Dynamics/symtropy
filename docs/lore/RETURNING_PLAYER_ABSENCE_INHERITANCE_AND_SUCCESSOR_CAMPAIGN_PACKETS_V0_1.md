# Returning Player, Absence, Inheritance, and Successor Campaign Packets

## Purpose

These packets author the emotional and political experience of returning to a place the player helped found after it has continued without them.

Return is not a reward ceremony. It is an encounter with other people's continuity.

> **The deepest proof that a settlement lives is that the player can come home and discover that home did not wait.**

# Packet 1 — The Founder's Key No Longer Works

## Situation

The player returns after five years and attempts to enter the old council archive with a founder credential.

The credential is cryptographically genuine and legally expired.

## Present Actors

- current archivist;
- successor council officer;
- old companion who remembers the original emergency;
- resident who lost privacy under the old access regime;
- IRIS, which can verify provenance but not restore authority.

## Possible Paths

- request public records normally;
- petition for historical access;
- seek consent from affected people;
- challenge the law;
- exploit an old physical vulnerability;
- accept that the archive is no longer theirs.

## Long Consequence

The case may establish a precedent separating historical identity from current access.

# Packet 2 — The Town Has Another Name

## Situation

The legal settlement name remains, but nearly everyone uses a different name that emerged during the player's absence.

The new name comes from a sanitation strike, not the founding event.

## Tension

Tourism boards preserve the founder-era name. Residents use the new one. Older companions disagree about whether changing maps erases history or corrects it.

## Player Opportunity

The player may use either name, ask why, support a dual-name process, defend the old name, or remain outside the dispute.

The UI must reflect speaker-specific naming.

# Packet 3 — The Successor Is Better at the Job

## Situation

A former apprentice now runs the workshop, utility, clinic, or office more effectively than the player did.

They changed procedures the player considered central.

## Conflict

The player may feel pride, displacement, resentment, or relief. Workers may be polite but uninterested in restoring old leadership.

## Strong Outcome

The player becomes a respected specialist without reclaiming control.

# Packet 4 — The Charter Amendment

## Situation

Residents amended a founding clause after it caused exclusion or administrative failure.

The player's original speech is still quoted by opponents and defenders.

## Possible Questions

- Did the player foresee the harm?
- Does admitting error matter?
- Is the amendment itself fair?
- Who was excluded from the new vote?
- Does the player have standing beyond ordinary residency?

# Packet 5 — The Public Service You Thought Was Temporary

## Situation

A temporary clinic, housing block, emergency pump, or transport route became indispensable.

Its improvised design now creates safety and access problems.

## Conflict

Preservation groups defend its history. Workers want replacement. Residents fear displacement. The player's name makes ordinary engineering decisions politically charged.

# Packet 6 — The Child Who Is Now an Officer

## Situation

A child known during founding is now an adult office-holder, professional, critic, or organizer.

They remember the player through household stories and one direct event.

## Boundary

The game must not frame adulthood as the player's achievement.

The person has relationships, training, failures, and politics independent of the player.

# Packet 7 — The Promise Nobody Agreed You Kept

## Situation

The player believes they fulfilled a founding promise. A household argues that the technically completed work failed its intended purpose.

Example:

- houses were built but remained unaffordable;
- water access existed but required inaccessible travel;
- records were preserved but privacy was violated;
- a memorial was completed against family wishes.

## Resolution

There may be repair, compensation, apology, continuing disagreement, or no available remedy.

# Packet 8 — Your Statue, Their Workers

## Situation

A monument credits the player for a public work completed by many people.

A worker-history campaign seeks to change it.

## Options

- support relabeling;
- donate private records;
- oppose alteration;
- ask for removal;
- remain silent;
- discover the proposed replacement also erases someone.

# Packet 9 — The House Is Occupied

## Situation

The player's former residence is now used by another household, a clinic, an archive, or a cooperative.

The legal basis depends on abandonment, emergency law, inheritance, or disputed death.

## Principle

A home is not ordinary loot, but neither is founder property permanently immune to history.

The packet must represent current occupants as people, not blockers.

# Packet 10 — The Reconstituted Founder

## Situation

The player returns after legal death and reconstitution.

The settlement accepts identity continuity for personhood but not for office or every property claim.

A successor has governed for years. A family member has inherited. An IRIS fork holds partial private memory.

## Questions

- Which obligations survive?
- Which relationships changed?
- Can two versions share authorship?
- What does restoration repair, and what can it not undo?

# Packet 11 — The Founder Was the Problem

## Situation

A respected later history argues that one of the player's successful systems normalized coercion, exclusion, surveillance, extraction, or ecological harm.

The critique is neither wholly true nor wholly false.

## Gameplay

- inspect records;
- hear affected people;
- see real benefits;
- confront missing evidence;
- support reform;
- defend the system;
- refuse symbolic guilt while accepting material repair;
- discover the reform creates new harm.

# Packet 12 — The Founder Was Not Important

## Situation

The settlement's public identity centers events, workers, migrations, and cultural changes that occurred after the player left.

The player appears in a small museum panel or not at all.

## Emotional Goal

Allow insignificance without humiliation.

The settlement's flourishing is evidence of successful continuity.

# Packet 13 — The Settlement Failed

## Situation

The player returns to an evacuated, fragmented, or dissolved settlement.

Possible causes include:

- ecological overshoot;
- route collapse;
- political conflict;
- better opportunities elsewhere;
- war;
- corporate pressure;
- founding design error;
- ordinary demographic change.

## Content

- ruins;
- salvage claims;
- diaspora households;
- records;
- surviving institutions elsewhere;
- people who do not want restoration;
- a remaining caretaker;
- ecological recovery.

Failure does not erase the people who lived there.

# Packet 14 — The Settlement Became Mobile

## Situation

Residents moved the settlement into vehicles, ships, modular habitats, or seasonal routes.

The player returns to the site and finds only foundations and a schedule showing where the community will be next.

The settlement did not die; the player's spatial assumption failed.

# Packet 15 — The Successor Government Rejects You

## Situation

The current government considers the player politically dangerous, legally liable, symbolically destabilizing, or associated with an opposition movement.

It may be authoritarian, prudent, paranoid, or several at once.

The player may have relationships inside the settlement without lawful entry.

# Packet 16 — Your Festival Survived Wrong

## Situation

A ritual or festival proposed by the player survives in a form they dislike.

It may be commercialized, politicized, religious, ironic, vulgar, or detached from its original meaning.

The player cannot restore meaning by author declaration.

# Packet 17 — The Companion Holds Office

## Situation

A former companion now holds public office.

Their friendship with the player creates conflict-of-interest scrutiny.

They may refuse private access, disclose a meeting, recuse themselves, or oppose the player publicly.

Their office remains theirs.

# Packet 18 — The Machine Remembers Another Founder

## Situation

A service machine or archive system attributes founding decisions to a different group than public memory does.

Its record is detailed but incomplete, damaged, or based on a legal definition of founding that excludes care work.

The dispute becomes about both evidence and categories.

# Packet 19 — The Founder's Debt

## Situation

A loan, resource requisition, environmental obligation, or public guarantee from founding remains unresolved.

Successors have paid part of the cost. Some consider the debt illegitimate. External claimants recognize the returned player as responsible.

# Packet 20 — Ordinary Dinner

## Situation

Nothing urgent happens.

The player eats with people who remember them, people born later, and people who do not care about founding history.

Conversation includes work, jokes, food, children, repairs, politics, and one awkward historical question.

## Purpose

This packet proves the world can carry emotional continuity without crisis.

# Production Rules

Every return sequence should include:

- at least one ordinary scene;
- one expired permission;
- one person changed independently;
- one material trace;
- one contested interpretation;
- one opportunity to listen without intervening;
- one consequence the player cannot reverse;
- one positive development not credited to the player.

# Anti-Patterns

Avoid:

- everyone lining up to thank or condemn the founder;
- return as automatic coronation;
- successors becoming villains to validate player leadership;
- children existing mainly to show time passage;
- all old friends remaining emotionally frozen;
- legal identity restoring every property and office;
- ruins with no diaspora or surviving history;
- a single canonical judgment of the player's legacy.

# Closing Principle

> **Returning home should feel like encountering a relationship, not loading an old save slot.**
