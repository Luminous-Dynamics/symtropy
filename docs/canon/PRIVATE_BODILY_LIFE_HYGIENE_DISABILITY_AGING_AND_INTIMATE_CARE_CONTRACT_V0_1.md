# Private Bodily Life, Hygiene, Disability, Aging, and Intimate Care Contract

## Purpose

Symtropy models living bodies. It therefore cannot treat care, hygiene, disability, aging, reproduction, pain, and bodily dependency as embarrassing gaps between “real” gameplay systems.

At the same time, bodily realism must not become humiliation simulation, voyeurism, or an endless maintenance tax.

## Core Principle

> **A body may require help without losing dignity, privacy, agency, competence, sexuality, or social importance.**

# 1. What the Game May Represent

The game may represent, with appropriate abstraction and content controls:

- washing and sanitation;
- menstruation and menstrual care;
- toileting and incontinence care;
- wound cleaning and dressing;
- feeding assistance;
- mobility assistance;
- chronic pain;
- fatigue;
- scarring and disfigurement;
- sensory disability;
- cognitive disability;
- prosthetic and assistive-device dependence;
- pregnancy, birth, miscarriage, infertility, abortion, and postpartum care;
- aging and cognitive decline;
- medication routines;
- intimate caregiving between family, partners, workers, and clinicians;
- body odor, sweat, dirt, blood, waste, and contamination when materially relevant.

Representation is contextual, not exhaustive. Most bodily processes remain summarized unless they create meaningful care, risk, logistics, privacy, or relationship consequences.

# 2. No Humiliation Economy

The game must not reward players for degrading dependent people.

Forbidden patterns include:

- comedy derived solely from disability or incontinence;
- treating care recipients as inventory;
- repeated forced exposure of private bodies;
- public shame meters;
- sexualized medical vulnerability;
- cleanliness as moral worth;
- neglect as an efficient default with no social or health consequence;
- “fixing” disability as the universal successful outcome;
- forcing the player to perform graphic care procedures for realism credentials.

Difficult care can be depicted without turning a person into the difficulty.

# 3. Privacy and Consent

Bodily information is scoped.

Possible scopes include:

```text
private
household-shared
care-confidential
emergency-necessary
work-accommodation
public-by-choice
institutional-statistical
```

Rules:

- diagnosis does not automatically become public knowledge;
- assistive needs may be disclosed without exposing full medical history;
- care access does not authorize unrelated memory access;
- unconscious or incapacitated care follows explicit emergency and supported-decision rules;
- a caregiver's convenience does not erase refusal;
- cameras and recording devices are restricted in private care contexts;
- IRIS may assist with care calculations but cannot consent for the patient.

# 4. Care as Skilled Labor

Care work consumes:

- time;
- training;
- attention;
- supplies;
- clean water;
- energy;
- privacy;
- emotional capacity;
- accessible space;
- backup coverage.

The game recognizes:

- professional caregivers;
- family caregivers;
- mutual-aid care;
- machine caregivers;
- self-care;
- community clinics;
- informal care networks.

Caregiver fatigue is real but does not imply resentment or abuse automatically.

Institutions must account for care labor instead of assuming it happens invisibly.

# 5. Disability Is Not One State

Disability emerges from the relationship among body, environment, tools, support, law, and social expectation.

Examples:

- a wheelchair user is disabled by stairs, narrow doors, inaccessible vehicles, and missing repair parts;
- a Deaf worker may be fully competent while an alarm system remains inaccessible;
- chronic pain may fluctuate rather than progress linearly;
- a machine citizen may require an obsolete port or sensory translator;
- an alien body may be disabled by human atmospheric or acoustic standards;
- a cognitively disabled person may require supported decision-making without surrendering legal personhood.

The game should permit competence with accommodation rather than presenting all disability as debuff.

# 6. Assistive Technology

Assistive devices are not cosmetic props.

They have:

- fit;
- maintenance;
- power or consumable needs;
- privacy implications;
- repair knowledge;
- supply chains;
- customization;
- failure modes;
- ownership and insurance politics.

Examples include:

- mobility devices;
- prosthetics;
- exoskeletal support;
- sensory translation;
- communication boards;
- medication delivery;
- cognitive reminders;
- environmental controls;
- body-firmware interfaces;
- custom seating and pressure management.

Losing an assistive device may be an emergency even when the person's underlying condition has not changed.

# 7. Aging

Aging is not a universal decay curve.

It may involve:

- accumulated expertise;
- slower recovery;
- changing sensory needs;
- social authority;
- retirement conflict;
- grief and memory;
- mobility changes;
- new care relationships;
- political exclusion;
- body modification choices;
- refusal of extension treatment;
- fear of becoming dependent;
- relief at leaving work.

Elders remain agents with projects, humor, desire, anger, and privacy.

# 8. Reproductive and Menstrual Health

Reproductive health is treated as ordinary healthcare, not a special moral minigame.

The game may represent:

- menstrual supplies and privacy;
- fertility treatment;
- pregnancy risk;
- prenatal care;
- birth planning;
- miscarriage and grief;
- abortion access;
- postpartum recovery;
- contraception;
- reproductive coercion;
- genetic and body-sovereignty decisions.

Rules:

- reproductive status is private;
- pregnancy never removes political or personal agency;
- abortion is not used as shock scenery;
- miscarriage is not a punishment mechanic;
- bodies do not become faction assets;
- minors are protected by the life-course and safeguarding contracts;
- player options must respect setting law and consent without declaring one metaphysical answer.

# 9. Hygiene and Sanitation

Hygiene becomes gameplay when it connects to:

- disease risk;
- comfort;
- dignity;
- water access;
- labor;
- shelter;
- contamination;
- social exclusion;
- medical treatment;
- infrastructure failure.

It should usually be represented through facility state, care plans, routines, and resource systems—not repetitive button presses.

The meaningful question is often not “Did the player wash?” but:

- Is clean water available?
- Does the shelter have private facilities?
- Who cleans after a crisis?
- Are workers protected?
- Can a disabled resident use the bathroom?
- What happens when hygiene supplies are rationed?

# 10. Intimate Care Scenes

Intimate care may be emotionally important.

Scenes should emphasize:

- negotiated help;
- routine competence;
- embarrassment without ridicule;
- trust;
- fatigue;
- privacy;
- practical detail;
- the recipient's preferences;
- the caregiver's boundaries;
- what happens afterward.

Content may be skipped, shortened, or represented through audio, text, or summary without changing outcome quality.

No critical success condition depends on watching private bodily detail.

# 11. Neglect and Abuse

Neglect may be caused by:

- resource shortage;
- caregiver exhaustion;
- inaccessible facilities;
- institutional understaffing;
- deliberate punishment;
- discrimination;
- coercive control;
- war or displacement.

The game distinguishes systemic failure, incapacity, negligence, and intentional abuse.

A dirty room is evidence requiring context, not automatic proof that one caregiver is evil.

# 12. Death, Bodies, and Aftercare

Dead bodies retain dignity, identity, evidentiary status, cultural meaning, and possible source-chain relevance.

Care after death may include:

- washing;
- identification;
- infection control;
- family access;
- ritual;
- evidence preservation;
- transport;
- burial, return, reclamation, or other culturally grounded practices.

Bodies do not become ordinary salvage.

# 13. Game Rhythm

Bodily realism must not overwhelm the primary game.

Use:

- routine automation;
- delegation;
- care plans;
- household schedules;
- facility-level summaries;
- meaningful exceptions;
- player-selected depth;
- accessibility settings;
- content warnings.

The player should encounter care as part of civilization, not spend every session managing every bodily process.

# 14. Authoring Requirements

A care-related character or scene must specify:

- the person's goals outside care;
- what help they want;
- what help they refuse;
- privacy boundaries;
- relevant skills and accommodations;
- who performs labor;
- material requirements;
- possible institutional failure;
- post-scene continuity.

The character must not exist solely to educate the player or display suffering.

# 15. Acceptance Failures

This contract fails if:

- care recipients become passive resources;
- disability is represented only as reduced stats;
- intimate care is mandatory spectacle;
- reproductive outcomes punish unrelated player morality;
- private medical information becomes public automatically;
- hygiene becomes repetitive survival busywork;
- aging erases desire, competence, or political agency;
- assistive devices lack material and repair realities;
- one cure is treated as the only dignified ending;
- neglect is attributed to one villain while systemic causes disappear.

## Closing Principle

> **Civilization is visible in how it handles the body when the body is inconvenient, private, aging, painful, dependent, or different from the assumed norm.**
