# Life Course, Households, Kinship, and Education Contract

## Owned Question

How can Symtropy represent people across years, generations, households, care relationships, education, migration, aging, and succession without reducing children, elders, families, or dependent people to resources in a settlement spreadsheet?

## Core Thesis

A living civilization contains people who are not currently optimized for production.

It contains:

- children who need protection and teaching;
- adolescents testing roles and boundaries;
- adults balancing work, care, love, obligation, and fatigue;
- elders carrying skill, memory, authority, dependence, and unfinished conflict;
- disabled people whose lives cannot be summarized as deficits;
- households that share resources without sharing every belief;
- chosen families, machine kin, collective persons, and nonhuman life cycles;
- people who arrive, leave, age, reproduce, adopt, inherit, retire, grieve, and die.

A settlement that simulates labor but not care is not simulating civilization.

## Prime Directive

> **People are not workforce slots passing through demographic states. Life stages create different rights, needs, powers, dependencies, risks, and forms of agency.**

# 1. Household Is a Relationship Structure, Not a Building

A household is a persistent coordination and care network.

It may include:

- biological relatives;
- chosen family;
- friends sharing survival obligations;
- multigenerational groups;
- communal households;
- foster and adoptive relationships;
- co-parenting networks;
- machine or uplifted kin;
- ritual kinship;
- temporary evacuation households;
- long-distance members linked by worldline or migration history.

Household membership does not imply:

- identical ownership;
- unconditional trust;
- romantic relationship;
- shared politics;
- authority over another member’s body;
- automatic access to private memory;
- permanent residence.

# 2. Canonical Life Stages

Life stages must be authored by species and body profile rather than assumed from one human template.

For humans and human-derived populations, useful broad stages include:

- infancy and early dependence;
- childhood;
- adolescence;
- emerging adulthood;
- established adulthood;
- later adulthood;
- elderhood;
- terminal care or end-of-life transition.

These are simulation bands, not rigid social truths.

For machine, alien, hybrid, and collective persons, stages may instead involve:

- fabrication and initialization;
- apprenticeship or calibration;
- distributed growth;
- molt or metamorphosis;
- fork stabilization;
- chorus integration;
- memory transfer;
- seasonal embodiment;
- retirement from hazardous service;
- archival continuity after body loss.

## 2.1 Life Stage Is Not Capability Rank

Age should not map directly to competence.

A child may know a local ecosystem better than an arriving engineer. An elder may carry irreplaceable tacit knowledge while needing mobility support. A newly embodied machine may have deep archived knowledge but no social calibration.

# 3. Rights Floor Across Life Stages

Every charter and worldline profile must protect a rights floor that cannot be traded for efficiency.

At minimum:

- protection from exploitation and coerced labor;
- bodily and cognitive privacy;
- access to care appropriate to body and life stage;
- safe shelter and evacuation priority based on need, not status;
- education or developmental opportunity;
- freedom from forced modification;
- relationship and family integrity subject to safety;
- appeal against guardianship, institutional control, or separation where the person can meaningfully participate;
- continuity of identity through reconstitution and migration;
- protection from being used as leverage in player conflict.

Children and other dependent people may never be designed as loot, trade goods, combat rewards, collectible labor, or romance targets.

# 4. Care Is a First-Class Economy

Care includes:

- feeding;
- medical support;
- emotional presence;
- supervision;
- teaching;
- mobility assistance;
- sensory accommodation;
- conflict mediation;
- rest protection;
- household administration;
- grief work;
- cultural transmission;
- maintenance of assistive devices;
- end-of-life support.

Care consumes time, energy, skill, space, and attention. It also creates trust, competence, resilience, attachment, and cultural continuity.

The simulation must not hide care labor behind an abstract happiness bonus.

## 4.1 Care Burden and Care Capacity

Households and institutions track bounded care commitments and capacity, but never assign a person a numeric worth.

A care crisis may arise from:

- too many simultaneous needs;
- illness or injury;
- worker exhaustion;
- inaccessible infrastructure;
- migration;
- school or clinic closure;
- loss of a caregiver;
- conflict or displacement;
- specialized body-maintenance dependency.

Responses can include mutual aid, schedule changes, public services, automation, respite, migration, institutional reform, or harmful abandonment.

# 5. Education and Development

Education is not a passive research-speed modifier.

It is how people learn:

- language;
- bodily regulation;
- tool use;
- repair practice;
- ecological knowledge;
- public reason;
- history;
- consent;
- self-advocacy;
- cultural forms;
- scientific method;
- machine or nonhuman communication;
- how to challenge authority safely.

Education may occur through:

- households;
- schools;
- apprenticeships;
- tool libraries;
- guilds;
- clinics;
- rituals;
- field expeditions;
- machine tutors;
- peer groups;
- public repair;
- archives;
- direct participation in settlement life.

No single institution should own all legitimate learning.

# 6. Adolescence, Experimentation, and Becoming

Adolescent characters need room to:

- try roles;
- form peer groups;
- question household beliefs;
- seek privacy;
- make bounded mistakes;
- learn tools under supervision;
- create art, fashion, games, and subcultures;
- participate in age-appropriate civic life;
- resist being treated only as future workers.

The player may mentor, protect, teach, listen, or create opportunities. The player must not receive coercive authority over identity, intimacy, body modification, or permanent allegiance.

# 7. Aging and Elderhood

Elderhood may bring:

- accumulated skill;
- social authority;
- slower recovery;
- altered sensory access;
- care needs;
- grief accumulation;
- long institutional memory;
- resistance to change;
- freedom from former obligations;
- new teaching or witnessing roles.

Aging should not be rendered solely as decline.

An elder may become more important to a settlement as direct labor capacity decreases. Conversely, age does not grant unquestionable authority.

# 8. Disability and Access

Disability is a relationship among bodies, environments, tools, institutions, and social expectations.

The simulation should model:

- assistive technology;
- accessible routes;
- communication alternatives;
- care preferences;
- fluctuating capacity;
- pain and fatigue without making them personality;
- expertise developed through lived adaptation;
- political conflict around access;
- the cost of inaccessible design.

The system must avoid treating every modification or cure as automatically desired.

# 9. Reproduction, Adoption, and New Persons

Reproduction and the creation of new persons are culturally and biologically varied.

Possible pathways include:

- pregnancy and birth;
- assisted reproduction;
- adoption;
- communal child-rearing;
- clone or body-template creation;
- machine fabrication and initialization;
- forked digital continuity;
- symbiotic assembly;
- alien metamorphosis;
- collective fission or budding.

These systems must remain abstract enough to avoid exploitative micromanagement while preserving consequences for care, housing, identity, inheritance, health, and culture.

No player action should force reproduction or person creation without explicit, valid in-world consent and the relevant rights protections.

# 10. Kinship, Guardianship, and Authority

Kinship creates obligation and attachment. It does not automatically create unrestricted authority.

Guardianship or substitute decision-making must be:

- scoped;
- reviewable;
- based on actual need;
- limited to relevant domains;
- challengeable where possible;
- recorded when it affects rights;
- prevented from becoming property ownership.

The simulation should distinguish:

- care responsibility;
- legal guardianship;
- household membership;
- emotional attachment;
- financial dependency;
- medical proxy;
- civic representation.

# 11. Inheritance and Succession

When a person dies, leaves, retires, forks, or becomes unavailable, their responsibilities and possessions do not vanish cleanly.

Possible succession objects include:

- tools;
- home access;
- debt and obligations;
- apprentices;
- institutional office;
- blueprint custody;
- unfinished projects;
- personal archives;
- machine maintenance keys;
- cultural roles;
- testimony;
- care commitments.

Succession may be determined by:

- explicit will or source-chain instruction;
- household agreement;
- charter law;
- guild procedure;
- ritual;
- public office rules;
- machine continuity protocol;
- dispute and appeal.

Inheritance cannot transfer ownership of persons, bodily dependencies, or private cognition.

# 12. Migration, Separation, and Reunion

Households may be divided by:

- evacuation;
- work routes;
- exile;
- war;
- education;
- space travel;
- worldline fork;
- quarantine;
- voluntary departure;
- death and reconstitution.

The simulation should preserve:

- relationship continuity;
- communication history;
- unresolved obligations;
- custody and access disputes;
- migration intentions;
- cultural adaptation;
- possible reunion or estrangement.

A returning person should encounter a household that changed while they were away.

# 13. Household Resources and Privacy

Households may coordinate:

- food;
- shelter;
- care schedules;
- shared tools;
- transport;
- energy;
- education;
- emergency plans;
- savings or mutual obligations.

They do not automatically share:

- every inventory item;
- every memory;
- every credential;
- every relationship;
- every political position;
- every room;
- every body-maintenance record.

Household interfaces must expose agreements and access scopes rather than assume total communal ownership.

# 14. Conflict Within Households

Household conflict may involve:

- unequal care work;
- money or resource use;
- political disagreement;
- unsafe work;
- migration;
- privacy;
- education;
- inheritance;
- prejudice;
- body modification;
- responsibility for a dependent member;
- grief;
- old betrayal.

The system must not romanticize family unity or assume reconciliation is always the best outcome. Safe separation may be a successful resolution.

# 15. Simulation Resolution

## Named households

Track members, commitments, private and shared resources, care dependencies, schedules, conflicts, projects, and durable memories.

## Situated households

Track aggregate care capacity, member locations, major obligations, and relationship events.

## Regional population groups

Track age and life-stage bands, household types, education access, care capacity, migration pressure, and institutional load without simulating every private relationship.

Aggregate simulation must conserve population and obligations while avoiding demographic determinism.

# 16. Representative Proof

The first proof should expand the four-NPC household into a small social ecology containing:

- one adolescent apprentice;
- one elder with valuable tacit knowledge and mobility needs;
- at least two household forms;
- one school or tool-library learning space;
- one care-capacity crisis;
- one succession or temporary guardianship decision;
- one voluntary departure or return.

The proof passes only if the younger and older characters have agency beyond need states, care work is visible, education changes capability through actual participation, and household privacy survives player inspection.

# 17. Acceptance Criteria

The contract is satisfied when:

- life stages alter needs, rights, roles, and expression without determining worth;
- households coordinate care and resources through explicit scopes;
- children and dependent people cannot become exploitable game assets;
- elders retain agency and knowledge;
- education creates observable learning trajectories;
- care labor has causal consequences;
- disability interacts with environment and access rather than functioning as a generic penalty;
- succession, migration, and reconstitution preserve relationships and obligations;
- private household state is protected;
- population LOD preserves consequential continuity.

## Final Rule

> **A civilization becomes believable when the people who cannot be reduced to immediate production still shape its future.**
