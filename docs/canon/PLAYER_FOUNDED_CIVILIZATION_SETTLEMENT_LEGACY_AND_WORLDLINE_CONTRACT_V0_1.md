# Player-Founded Civilization, Settlement Legacy, and Worldline Contract

## Owned Question

**How can a player help found, transform, and remember a civilization without becoming its owner, unquestioned sovereign, sole author, or permanent center of history?**

## Core Thesis

A player-founded settlement is not a base that has been decorated with citizens.

It is a living polity whose inhabitants possess prior histories, present needs, rights, obligations, disagreements, and futures that do not belong to the player.

The player may become a founder, builder, office-holder, patron, witness, organizer, emergency steward, cultural figure, exile, returning ancestor, or contested historical person. None of those roles grants metaphysical ownership of the settlement.

> **The player may help begin a civilization. The civilization must be able to continue, reinterpret, reject, or outgrow them.**

## Prime Directives

1. **No empty land fiction.** Every site has ecological, historical, legal, cultural, infrastructural, or nonhuman claims, even when no permanent settlement is visible.
2. **Founding is collective.** A viable settlement requires households, workers, institutions, material flows, care systems, and legitimate participation.
3. **Construction does not equal sovereignty.** Building a clinic, road, habitat, or power plant does not automatically confer ownership over residents or government.
4. **Rights precede convenience.** Emergency founding may temporarily narrow procedure, but it may not silently erase bodily autonomy, due process, exit, privacy, or basic survival rights.
5. **Charters remain amendable.** A founding compact is a public commitment and operating scaffold, not sacred code immune to later generations.
6. **Institutions must survive personalities.** Public services, records, budgets, maintenance, and authority require succession paths.
7. **Culture requires adoption.** A player may propose names, symbols, rituals, art, and laws. Other people decide whether to use, alter, mock, commercialize, or reject them.
8. **Failure remains historical.** Abandoned works, broken promises, bankrupt institutions, and failed settlements leave traces rather than resetting into empty build space.
9. **Absence is real.** Settlements continue while the player travels, dies, disconnects, or returns after long delay.
10. **Legacy is plural.** Different groups may remember the same founder as protector, exploiter, fool, ancestor, landlord, absentee, technician, myth, or irrelevant early participant.
11. **Multiplayer founding requires shared authority.** No host or first placer silently acquires permanent political control.
12. **Worldline branches preserve ancestry.** Forked settlements share history without being forced into one canonical future.

# 1. What Counts as Founding

Founding is the transition from temporary presence to durable shared obligations.

It can begin through:

- emergency refuge;
- invited settlement;
- reconstruction of a damaged town;
- worker occupation of abandoned infrastructure;
- secession from an existing polity;
- a mobile convoy becoming stationary;
- an orbital or planetary habitat reaching civic scale;
- a scientific station acquiring households and public services;
- a religious, artistic, or cooperative commune;
- a postwar resettlement;
- a machine or mixed-species community seeking recognition;
- an ecological stewardship settlement with limited human claims.

A campsite does not become a civilization merely because it contains beds and storage.

The founding threshold requires at least:

```text
people who expect continuity
recognized responsibilities
survival infrastructure
rules for shared risk
methods for resolving disagreement
care for those unable to contribute immediately
records or memory practices
an answer to who may leave and what they may take
an answer to what the settlement owes its site and neighbors
```

# 2. Founding Legitimacy

Legitimacy is multidimensional and may remain contested.

## 2.1 Sources of Legitimacy

- invitation by existing residents;
- emergency necessity;
- inherited or treaty-recognized claims;
- labor that restores indispensable infrastructure;
- democratic ratification;
- ecological stewardship obligations;
- negotiated use with neighboring polities;
- recognized refuge duties;
- continuity from a prior lawful institution;
- public trust earned through repeated service.

No single source automatically overrides all others.

A settlement may be legally recognized but ecologically destructive. It may be materially indispensable but democratically weak. It may be invited by one group and opposed by another.

## 2.2 Prior Claims

Before settlement expansion, the simulation must search for:

- resident households;
- seasonal use;
- sacred or memorial use;
- migration corridors;
- subsurface or watershed effects;
- prior abandonment under coercion;
- disputed ownership;
- machine stewardship;
- nonhuman agency;
- treaty restrictions;
- contamination or unresolved hazard;
- public-service dependencies extending beyond the site.

"Unoccupied" is an observation about visible presence, not proof of absence of claim.

## 2.3 Emergency Founding

Emergency settlements may use provisional authority when delay would cause serious harm.

Provisional measures require:

- stated scope;
- expiration or review date;
- public record;
- minimum-rights floor;
- appeal route;
- transition plan;
- accounting of seized or consumed resources;
- restoration or compensation obligations.

Emergency authority that renews itself without review becomes a constitutional crisis, not a permanent gameplay buff.

# 3. Player Roles

The player may occupy several roles over time.

## 3.1 Founding Organizer

Coordinates people, site, resources, and early agreements.

Power comes from work and trust, not automatic title.

## 3.2 Technical Founder

Builds or restores essential systems but may hold little political authority.

A brilliant engineer can be indispensable and still lose an election.

## 3.3 Charter Delegate

Represents a household, crew, faction, sponsor, or constituency in founding negotiations.

The player may not claim to represent people who did not authorize them.

## 3.4 Emergency Steward

Exercises narrow temporary authority under declared conditions.

This role must end, transform, or be renewed through legitimate procedure.

## 3.5 Elected or Appointed Officer

Holds a bounded office with duties, records, conflicts of interest, and removal conditions.

Office is not companion loyalty, settlement ownership, or permanent player progression.

## 3.6 Patron or Investor

Provides capital, materials, ships, land access, or technology.

Investment may create repayment claims but not ownership of persons, public institutions, air, water, medical access, or political voice beyond agreed rules.

## 3.7 Resident Without Office

The player may live, work, vote, organize, and care for others without governing.

This must be a complete and rewarding playstyle.

## 3.8 Departed Founder

A founder who leaves may retain relationships and historical meaning but loses day-to-day authority unless a continuing office or mandate remains legitimate.

## 3.9 Returning Historical Person

After long absence, death, or reconstitution, the player returns to a society that may not recognize their old permissions, interpretation, property, or identity claims.

# 4. Founding Phases

## Phase A — Intent

The initiating group states why settlement is being attempted.

Possible intents include refuge, work, restoration, research, worship, trade, defense, autonomy, care, ecological stewardship, or profit.

Intent is not destiny. Later residents may change the settlement's purpose.

## Phase B — Site and Claim Review

The group investigates prior claims, hazards, resource flows, neighbors, and ecological boundaries.

A rushed review may create delayed conflict rather than immediate failure.

## Phase C — Provisional Compact

The compact defines:

- membership and residency;
- authority;
- emergency powers;
- minimum rights;
- resource contribution;
- work expectations;
- care obligations;
- conflict process;
- review date;
- exit and portability.

## Phase D — Survival and Public Service

The settlement must establish or negotiate access to:

- water or equivalent life support;
- energy;
- sanitation;
- shelter;
- food;
- medical care;
- communications;
- mobility;
- waste handling;
- safety;
- records;
- repair capacity.

Decorative growth without public-service capacity produces a fragile camp, not a stable city.

## Phase E — Charter Convention

Once immediate survival no longer justifies purely provisional rule, residents confront long-term questions.

The convention may be formal, improvised, traditional, machine-mediated, multi-species, or distributed. It must expose who can participate, whose absence matters, and how dissent is recorded.

## Phase F — Institutionalization

Offices, budgets, procedures, courts, councils, cooperatives, guilds, assemblies, or other durable structures begin operating.

## Phase G — Succession

The settlement proves it can transfer responsibility, amend rules, survive leadership loss, and maintain public services.

Only after succession should the simulation call the founding institutionally mature.

# 5. Minimum Rights Floor

Every player-founded settlement must explicitly address:

- bodily autonomy;
- access to survival essentials;
- freedom from forced labor;
- consent and adult status;
- privacy and data access;
- due process;
- protection from arbitrary expulsion;
- practical exit;
- family and dependent continuity;
- disability accommodation;
- emergency care;
- source-chain and identity protections;
- machine and nonhuman standing where applicable;
- freedom of belief and nonbelief;
- appeal and public-record access.

A charter may fail these rights. The game may allow oppressive settlements to emerge. It must represent the mechanisms, beneficiaries, victims, resistance, and consequences rather than treating authoritarian clauses as flavor text.

# 6. Property and Commons

The founding contract must distinguish:

- personal property;
- household property;
- cooperative assets;
- public infrastructure;
- common resources;
- sacred or protected sites;
- leased assets;
- corporate or external claims;
- abandoned property;
- ecological systems that cannot be owned in ordinary terms.

Placing an object does not settle every ownership question.

A player-built pump may become public infrastructure. A privately funded clinic may acquire public-service obligations. A communal workshop may reject privatization after the player leaves. A home may remain personal even when the settlement needs its land.

# 7. Naming and Symbolic Authority

The player may propose:

- settlement names;
- district names;
- flags;
- seals;
- uniforms;
- monuments;
- holidays;
- songs;
- public mottos;
- building dedications.

The simulation must record:

- proposer;
- adoption process;
- constituencies using it;
- rival names;
- slang;
- later reinterpretation;
- commercialization;
- rejection;
- legal versus colloquial status.

A map label is not proof that everyone accepts the name.

# 8. Settlement Memory

A player-founded civilization remembers through:

- public records;
- household stories;
- work logs;
- architecture;
- songs;
- legal precedent;
- ruins;
- rituals;
- school curricula;
- jokes;
- debts;
- injuries;
- ecological change;
- machine memory;
- IRIS records;
- external histories.

Memory is not a single reputation score.

The player may be remembered differently by workers, descendants, rivals, migrants, machines, neighboring ecologies, and institutions.

# 9. Succession and Founder Limits

Every founding path must answer:

- Who takes over when a founder leaves?
- What permissions expire?
- Which records are public?
- Which obligations transfer?
- How can residents remove an officer?
- Can a founder be tried, recalled, or barred?
- What happens to founder-owned infrastructure?
- How are heirs or forks treated?
- Can the settlement rename itself?
- Can it dissolve?

A founder may receive honors without retaining authority.

A founder's house may become a museum, clinic, private home, demolished structure, contested shrine, or ordinary property. None is guaranteed.

# 10. Absence, Death, and Reconstitution

When the player is absent, the settlement continues through:

- maintenance;
- elections;
- births and deaths;
- migration;
- debt;
- conflict;
- ecological change;
- institutional drift;
- cultural adoption;
- external pressure;
- companion projects.

After death or reconstitution, the returned player may face:

- identity verification;
- expired office;
- disputed property;
- altered relationships;
- source-chain gaps;
- multiple plausible successors;
- a society that considers the original founder dead;
- a society that accepts continuity but rejects old authority.

Being recognized as the same person does not automatically restore an old office.

# 11. Worldline Branches

A player-founded settlement may branch through:

- constitutional amendment;
- secession;
- evacuation;
- migration;
- forked governance;
- multiplayer divergence;
- reconstitution disputes;
- alternative historical simulations;
- incompatible save ancestry.

Each branch must preserve:

- common ancestry;
- branch point;
- inherited obligations;
- altered institutions;
- distinct population history;
- non-transferable authority.

The game must not declare one branch morally or ontologically real merely because it belongs to the host.

# 12. Anti-Failure Conditions

The system fails this contract if:

- residents appear only after the player places buildings;
- the player owns public institutions by construction default;
- charters are cosmetic perk menus;
- culture changes instantly when the player selects a symbol;
- absence freezes the settlement;
- founder reputation is a single scalar;
- elected office becomes permanent progression;
- dissent exists only as enemy spawning;
- oppressive rules provide bonuses without lived consequences;
- prior claims vanish after scanning;
- every successful settlement converges on one optimal constitution;
- the player can dismiss residents from their own city as inventory management;
- returning founders automatically regain authority.

# 13. Promotion Gates

This design remains **D3 / I0** until a representative implementation proves:

- prior-claim discovery;
- collective founding participation;
- a provisional compact;
- one amendable charter;
- three public services with real operating costs;
- one office transfer;
- one lawful refusal of founder authority;
- one five-year absence;
- one renamed or reinterpreted institution;
- one founder legacy dispute;
- one worldline branch;
- deterministic replay and multiplayer authority safety.

# Closing Principle

> **A base belongs to the player. A civilization belongs to its inhabitants, its dependencies, its dead, its neighbors, and its future.**
