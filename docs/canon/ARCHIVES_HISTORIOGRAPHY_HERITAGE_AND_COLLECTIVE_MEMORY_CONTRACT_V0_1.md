# Archives, Historiography, Heritage, and Collective Memory Contract

## Owned Question

How does Symtropy let societies preserve, contest, lose, reinterpret, repair, and inherit history without reducing truth to a perfect database or memory to decorative lore?

## Core Thesis

A Chronicle event is not the same thing as a complete history.

Archives preserve evidence. People interpret it. Institutions classify it. Communities remember what records omit. Power decides what is funded, translated, displayed, hidden, destroyed, or treated as authoritative.

> **History is durable when evidence survives, interpretations remain contestable, and living communities can challenge the story without silently rewriting the record.**

Symtropy must distinguish:

```text
physical trace
sensor record
signed event
administrative record
personal testimony
oral history
ritual memory
public narrative
historical interpretation
propaganda
forgery
missing evidence
unknown past
```

# 1. Archive Is an Institution and an Infrastructure System

An archive may be:

- a public record hall;
- a distributed Chronicle mirror;
- a household collection;
- a machine memory court;
- an oral-history network;
- a ritual lineage;
- a species memory ecology;
- a ship black box;
- a genetic vault;
- an archaeological site;
- a diaspora repository;
- a planetary sensor archive;
- a contested private collection.

Every archive has:

```text
custody
access rules
preservation conditions
classification rules
indexing and translation
resource requirements
known omissions
authority claims
appeal paths
failure modes
```

Archives require power, space, media maintenance, climate control, translation, cataloging, redundancy, and people who understand context.

# 2. Evidence Layers

## 2.1 Primary Evidence

Direct traces such as:

- signed source-chain records;
- device transactions;
- physical artifacts;
- biological remains;
- environmental scars;
- original testimony;
- images or sensor streams;
- contemporaneous records;
- machine logs.

Primary does not mean infallible. Sensors fail, people lie, records omit, signatures prove authorship rather than truth, and artifacts lose context.

## 2.2 Derived Evidence

Examples:

- reconstructions;
- statistical summaries;
- translated records;
- forensic models;
- provenance chains;
- oral-history compilations;
- archaeological interpretations.

Derived evidence must preserve its method and uncertainty.

## 2.3 Public Narrative

A society may teach or commemorate a simplified story. Public narratives can create solidarity, mourning, denial, pride, or exclusion.

The game must never equate public consensus with historical certainty.

# 3. Historiography Is Playable Interpretation

Historiography is the process by which actors decide:

- which questions matter;
- which evidence is credible;
- whose testimony is included;
- what context is necessary;
- which causal claims are justified;
- what remains uncertain;
- how the past should affect present obligations.

A historical dispute should not become a dialogue puzzle with one hidden correct sentence.

It should involve:

```text
evidence discovery
provenance checking
translation
missing records
conflicting testimony
institutional incentives
community memory
material consequences
public interpretation
possible repair
```

# 4. Archive Authority and Rights

Archives may hold dangerous power over:

- identity;
- citizenship;
- ownership;
- inheritance;
- criminal accusation;
- medical history;
- migration claims;
- machine continuity;
- land and water rights;
- treaty obligations;
- historical blame;
- access to sacred or private knowledge.

The rights floor must include:

- correction without erasure of the original record;
- access to records used against a person where safety permits;
- protection of medical, intimate, cognitive, and child records;
- appeal of classification and access denial;
- protection against identity loss from archive failure;
- community participation in culturally sensitive records;
- refusal of public spectacle for remains, trauma, and private testimony;
- preservation of uncertainty.

# 5. Forgetting Is Sometimes Necessary

Not every datum should be permanent or public.

Legitimate forgetting may include:

- expiration of minor disciplinary records;
- deletion of raw biometric data after its authorized use;
- privacy-preserving aggregation;
- sealed intimate correspondence;
- protected witness identity;
- removal of malicious duplicate media;
- de-indexing without falsifying historical existence;
- culturally required return, burial, or destruction.

The contract distinguishes:

```text
forgetting
privacy
sealing
redaction
de-indexing
destruction
erasure
falsification
```

These are not interchangeable.

# 6. Heritage Is a Living Claim

Heritage includes places, objects, practices, languages, routes, species relations, machines, music, food, craft, stories, and landscapes that communities understand as carrying continuity.

Heritage design must ask:

- who claims it;
- who was excluded from that claim;
- who maintains it;
- whether continued use conflicts with preservation;
- whether a ruin should be repaired, adapted, left scarred, returned, or dismantled;
- whether tourism, extraction, or public display causes harm;
- how displaced or descendant communities participate.

Heritage is not automatically old, beautiful, or morally good.

A checkpoint, factory, prison, company town, military memorial, or Null machine can become contested heritage.

# 7. Memorialization

Memorials may:

- name the dead;
- preserve warning;
- offer mourning space;
- support ritual;
- assert ownership;
- legitimize a regime;
- conceal victims;
- freeze one interpretation;
- become sites of protest or repair.

Players may help determine:

- whose names appear;
- whether uncertainty is shown;
- whether perpetrators and institutions are named;
- how remains are treated;
- whether the site remains functional;
- who controls ceremonies;
- what maintenance obligations follow.

A memorial should create future interactions rather than conclude history permanently.

# 8. Reparative History

Historical evidence can create present obligations.

Examples:

- returning stolen land or artifacts;
- restoring citizenship;
- repairing a polluted district;
- compensating coerced labor;
- reopening a buried investigation;
- recognizing a machine or nonhuman witness;
- correcting a public curriculum;
- restoring a language or place name;
- funding care for inherited harm;
- renegotiating an unjust treaty.

Reparative history must not imply that one payment or ceremony erases harm.

It may change institutions, resources, relationships, and future rights while leaving disagreement or grief.

# 9. Archive Failure Modes

Archives can fail through:

```text
physical destruction
media decay
power loss
format obsolescence
missing keys
translation loss
misclassification
underfunding
capture
censorship
forgery
data flood
context collapse
privacy breach
propaganda
ritual exclusivity
Null preservation logic
```

Null-Archive occurs when preservation rules become uninterruptible and living people cannot correct, contextualize, appeal, or access the records governing them.

# 10. Alien and Nonhuman Memory

Nonhuman memory may exist in:

- ecological succession;
- mineral stress;
- chemical gradients;
- migration paths;
- chorus repetition;
- machine continuity;
- body inheritance;
- orbital patterns;
- dream or symbolic spaces;
- distributed swarm configuration.

Humans may destroy records by treating them as terrain, weather, waste, noise, or biology.

First-contact archive work must preserve agency uncertainty and avoid automatic extraction.

# 11. Player-Facing Modes

## SCAN

Reveals material condition and possible traces.

## DIAG

Reveals preservation state, damage, format, and technical dependencies.

## ARCHIVE

Shows records, provenance, known gaps, versions, and classifications.

## CIVIC

Shows legal use, custody, access, appeal, and present obligations.

## NULL

Shows suspicious reinforcement, unexplained certainty, recursive redaction, provenance breaks, and dead authority.

## WITNESS

Allows the player to contribute bounded testimony without automatically establishing truth.

# 12. Seedworks Representative Scope

The representative proof should include:

- one damaged district archive;
- one official record;
- one oral-history contradiction;
- one physical artifact;
- one privacy-restricted record;
- one missing-context warning;
- one public narrative that is incomplete but not wholly false;
- one decision about preservation, access, or repair;
- one future consequence after the player leaves.

# 13. Acceptance Tests

The archive system fails if:

1. one signed record is treated as omniscient truth;
2. a correction silently overwrites the original;
3. private medical or intimate records become quest loot;
4. oral testimony is automatically inferior or automatically decisive;
5. uncertainty disappears when evidence is incomplete;
6. heritage is assigned without a community or maintenance relation;
7. memorial construction ends all disagreement;
8. historical repair resets reputation or grief universally;
9. procedural generation creates evidence without provenance;
10. a worldline fork loses the ancestry of contested records.

# 14. Non-Goals

This contract does not require:

- complete simulation of academic history;
- a universal truth court;
- permanent storage of every event;
- player access to all records;
- one correct memorial style;
- exhaustive museum management;
- perfect recovery of the past.

## Final Rule

> **The past should constrain the present through evidence and obligation, but never become an unchallengeable machine for ruling the living.**
