# Cultural Evolution, Language, and Intergenerational Transmission Contract

## Owned Question

How can cultures in Symtropy change over years and generations through teaching, migration, invention, conflict, environment, technology, memory, and youth agency without becoming fixed faction skins or random aesthetic mutation?

## Core Thesis

Culture is not a static trait attached to a settlement.

It is the changing collection of practices through which people make life meaningful, teach the young, mark boundaries, coordinate work, remember loss, express desire, challenge power, and adapt to new bodies and worlds.

> **Culture survives by changing, but change is never neutral: someone teaches it, contests it, forgets it, profits from it, revives it, or is excluded by it.**

# 1. Culture Is a Bundle of Practices

A culture may include:

```text
language and dialect
food
music
ritual
clothing
body presentation
architecture
humor
games
work etiquette
care practices
courtship and relationship forms
mourning
timekeeping
education
repair traditions
stories and historical narratives
sacred boundaries
public celebrations
conflict norms
```

No culture should be represented only by ideology, costume, or bonuses.

# 2. Cultural State Is Layered

Every person may participate in multiple cultural layers:

- household;
- neighborhood;
- profession or guild;
- age cohort;
- religion or belief community;
- migration or diaspora network;
- species or body culture;
- machine lineage;
- language community;
- faction;
- settlement;
- regional or planetary culture;
- worldline identity.

These layers may support or contradict each other.

# 3. Transmission Channels

Culture is transmitted through:

- caregivers;
- peers;
- schools;
- apprenticeships;
- rituals;
- media;
- festivals;
- architecture;
- food and domestic work;
- songs and stories;
- tools and maintenance practice;
- law and public procedure;
- migration;
- military service;
- trade;
- translation;
- machines and archives;
- player-authored creations.

Transmission is not copying. Learners reinterpret what they receive.

# 4. Language and Dialect

Language systems should represent:

- primary and secondary languages;
- literacy by register;
- technical vocabularies;
- dialect and accent;
- sign and gesture languages;
- machine protocols;
- nonhuman sensory communication;
- translation confidence;
- prestige and stigma;
- code-switching;
- language loss and revival.

Language difference should create perspective, intimacy, misunderstanding, humor, and access needs—not tedious universal comprehension penalties.

## 4.1 Translation Is Situated

Translation may fail because of:

- missing concepts;
- technical specialization;
- power differences;
- taboo;
- sensory mismatch;
- historical trauma;
- deliberate ambiguity;
- different agency structures;
- incompatible time scales.

A translation system must preserve uncertainty rather than replacing every phrase with perfect universal speech.

# 5. Cultural Change Drivers

Cultural practice changes through:

```text
generational reinterpretation
migration and intermarriage
new bodies or environments
technological change
war and occupation
disaster
resource change
religious reform
political revolution
commercialization
media concentration
education policy
language contact
alien contact
player authorship
memorialization
institutional suppression
```

The same driver may produce revival, fusion, resistance, abandonment, or fragmentation.

# 6. Youth and New-Generation Agency

Young people are not passive containers for tradition.

They may:

- create slang;
- remix rituals;
- reject inherited prejudice;
- revive an old craft;
- form subcultures;
- adapt machine or alien practices;
- challenge language hierarchies;
- commercialize or trivialize sacred forms;
- build new games, fashions, music, and political identities.

Adults may respond with pride, confusion, fear, regulation, participation, or repression.

# 7. Assimilation, Integration, and Cultural Pressure

Integration must not mean erasure.

Cultural pressure may come from:

- public-language requirements;
- school curricula;
- employment norms;
- housing patterns;
- media dominance;
- ritual calendars;
- dress codes;
- body standards;
- citizenship tests;
- platform interfaces;
- machine protocol defaults;
- military or emergency administration.

A society may provide common coordination while protecting minority practice, or it may demand conformity under the language of efficiency.

# 8. Cultural Appropriation and Exchange

Cross-cultural adoption is not automatically theft or harmony.

The game should consider:

- consent and relationship;
- sacred or restricted status;
- attribution;
- economic benefit;
- power asymmetry;
- distortion;
- participation of source communities;
- whether the practice remains alive or is treated as an artifact.

Players should be able to support respectful exchange, exploit a style, misunderstand it, or help create new hybrid traditions.

# 9. Cultural Institutions

Institutions that preserve and transform culture include:

- schools;
- archives;
- kitchens;
- festivals;
- media;
- places of worship;
- guilds;
- theaters;
- sports clubs;
- language councils;
- tool libraries;
- memorial organizations;
- households;
- artistic collectives;
- machine memory orders.

These institutions require labor, space, resources, legitimacy, and succession.

# 10. Ritual Drift

Rituals change when:

- original conditions disappear;
- new bodies participate;
- younger generations reinterpret symbols;
- an institution standardizes practice;
- commercialization changes access;
- disaster gives a ritual new meaning;
- a worldline fork creates competing anniversaries;
- alien contact changes the category of personhood or witness.

A ritual may become more inclusive, more rigid, more aesthetic, more political, or less understood.

# 11. Cultural Loss and Revival

Loss may follow:

- displacement;
- death of teachers;
- archive destruction;
- language stigma;
- inaccessible environments;
- prohibition;
- economic pressure;
- body modification;
- worldline migration;
- generational disinterest.

Revival requires more than finding an artifact.

It may require:

- living teachers;
- reconstructed context;
- new learners;
- space and time;
- language access;
- community consent;
- adaptation to present bodies and conditions;
- acknowledgment of uncertainty.

# 12. Cultural Authorship and Player Creation

Players may create:

- music;
- buildings;
- public art;
- clothing;
- rituals;
- festivals;
- games;
- charters;
- memorials;
- language tools;
- school curricula;
- media.

Player-created cultural forms become world culture only through adoption, repetition, institutions, adaptation, and memory. Uploading an asset does not automatically make it socially important.

# 13. Cultural Conflict

Conflict may arise over:

- public symbols;
- language access;
- sacred sites;
- school content;
- memorials;
- dress and body norms;
- festival resources;
- artistic ownership;
- burial and reconstitution;
- machine or nonhuman participation;
- commercialization;
- historical interpretation.

These conflicts should permit compromise, parallel practice, refusal, schism, reform, or continued disagreement.

# 14. No Cultural Morality Score

The simulation may track:

- participation;
- transmission;
- exclusion;
- conflict;
- vitality;
- resource support;
- language competence;
- institutional continuity;
- public visibility;
- coercion risk.

It must not assign a hidden value for cultural superiority, purity, authenticity, or progress.

# 15. Seedworks Representative Scope

The representative proof should include:

- two households with overlapping but different traditions;
- one adolescent subculture;
- one elder-held practice at risk of loss;
- one migrant language barrier;
- one public festival or ritual;
- one disputed adaptation;
- one player-created cultural contribution;
- one later reinterpretation after absence or crisis.

# 16. Acceptance Tests

Fail if:

1. culture is one settlement tag;
2. young people only inherit adult beliefs;
3. language difference becomes repetitive inconvenience;
4. translation is universally perfect;
5. cultural vitality is reduced to attendance numbers;
6. revival requires only collecting an item;
7. a player can own a culture by crafting its objects;
8. minority practice disappears automatically under majority preference;
9. ritual conflict has one hidden correct ideology;
10. worldline forks erase shared cultural ancestry.

# 17. Non-Goals

This contract does not require:

- simulation of every linguistic feature;
- procedural generation of complete natural languages;
- universal cultural harmony;
- fixed authenticity judgments;
- unrestricted user-generated content in every worldline;
- culture as constant political conflict.

## Final Rule

> **A culture should feel alive because people inherit it imperfectly, argue about it, adapt it to new worlds, and make it worth passing on.**
