# Civic Succession, Public Service, and Institutional Continuity Contract

## Owned Question

How does a Symtropy society continue functioning when founders die, leaders leave, experts retire, records conflict, offices are captured, emergencies interrupt normal procedure, or a new generation no longer accepts inherited authority?

## Core Thesis

A settlement is not institutionally alive because it has a charter or a leader.

It is alive when authority can be transferred, questioned, constrained, taught, audited, interrupted, and renewed without destroying the services people depend on.

> **Civilization survives leadership change when competence, authority, memory, and accountability are not trapped in the same person.**

The game must distinguish:

- a person from an office;
- an office from a service;
- a service from the institution that currently administers it;
- formal authority from practical competence;
- emergency command from permanent sovereignty;
- continuity from obedience;
- succession from inheritance of ownership.

# 1. Four Forms of Continuity

Every important institution must define four separable continuities.

## 1.1 Service Continuity

Can water, care, power, transport, defense, records, education, and food distribution continue during leadership disruption?

## 1.2 Authority Continuity

Who may make bounded decisions after an officeholder becomes unavailable, dies, is removed, resigns, defects, or loses legitimacy?

## 1.3 Knowledge Continuity

Where are procedures, tacit skills, contacts, warnings, exceptions, and unfinished obligations preserved?

## 1.4 Legitimacy Continuity

Why should people recognize the successor, interim process, or replacement institution as entitled to act?

A society may preserve one and lose another.

Examples:

```text
Service continues, but emergency authority never expires.
Authority transfers cleanly, but the only technician leaves.
Records survive, but nobody trusts their completeness.
A popular leader remains legitimate, but cannot administer a growing city.
```

# 2. Office Is a Bounded Authority Object

A public office must have:

```text
office_id
purpose
jurisdiction
authority_scopes
authority_limits
term_or_review_rule
eligibility_rules
appointment_or_selection_procedure
removal_procedure
interim_successor_rule
records_obligation
conflict_of_interest_rules
appeal_path
continuity_plan
```

Holding an office does not grant general control over the settlement.

An energy coordinator may allocate emergency load within a declared scope. They do not automatically gain police authority, archive access, reproductive authority, or ownership of the grid.

## 2.1 Separation of Powers Must Be Physical

Where appropriate, separation of powers should exist in world systems:

- separate credentials;
- distinct terminals;
- co-signature requirements;
- public logs;
- independent maintenance teams;
- appeal desks;
- witness ports;
- time-limited emergency tokens;
- hardware interlocks;
- distributed archives.

The player should be able to see when authority has become overconcentrated.

# 3. Succession Is a Process, Not a Rename

A succession event may be triggered by:

- death;
- retirement;
- resignation;
- incapacity;
- migration;
- loss of confidence;
- recall;
- conviction or accountability finding;
- disappearance;
- worldline fork;
- institutional merger;
- emergency displacement;
- machine continuity failure;
- alien or collective-person lifecycle transition.

A valid succession must resolve:

```text
Who performs urgent duties now?
Who possesses the office credentials?
Which records and obligations transfer?
Which powers expire rather than transfer?
What unfinished decisions remain open?
Who may challenge the transition?
What expertise is missing?
What public explanation is given?
```

## 3.1 Interim Authority

Interim authority must be visibly temporary.

It requires:

- defined scope;
- expiry or review trigger;
- public notice;
- restricted credential set;
- succession task list;
- preserved appeal rights;
- prohibition on irreversible self-entrenchment unless independently authorized.

## 3.2 Contested Succession

A contested succession should create playable consequences without automatically producing civil war.

Possible outcomes:

- dual administrations;
- service-specific recognition;
- temporary caretaker council;
- public hearing;
- regional mediation;
- strike or non-cooperation;
- archive dispute;
- negotiated split;
- charter amendment;
- exile;
- coup;
- worldline fork.

# 4. Public Service as Persistent Capability

Civil servants, operators, teachers, caregivers, technicians, archivists, dispatchers, inspectors, translators, and maintenance crews are not scenery behind charismatic leaders.

They preserve everyday institutional capacity.

Public-service gameplay should include:

- routine work;
- staffing gaps;
- handover;
- training;
- fatigue;
- professional norms;
- whistleblowing;
- procedural resistance;
- compassionate discretion;
- bureaucratic cruelty;
- recordkeeping;
- cross-department coordination;
- institutional reform.

## 4.1 Competence Is Distributed

No institution should depend on one irreplaceable genius without making that fragility explicit.

Continuity can be improved through:

- apprenticeships;
- runbooks;
- cross-training;
- public schematics;
- drills;
- rotating duties;
- shadow roles;
- machine assistance;
- regional mutual-aid agreements;
- succession rehearsals.

## 4.2 Bureaucracy Is Neither Automatically Bad nor Good

Bureaucracy can:

- preserve equal treatment;
- maintain memory;
- protect against arbitrary power;
- coordinate complexity;
- slow urgent care;
- hide responsibility;
- become inaccessible;
- protect incumbents;
- enforce dead rules.

The game should present bureaucracy as accumulated coordination technology with real benefits and failure modes.

# 5. Patronage, Capture, and Corruption

Corruption is not one morality meter.

It may involve:

- bribery;
- favoritism;
- nepotism;
- procurement capture;
- credential trading;
- selective enforcement;
- record suppression;
- conflict of interest;
- revolving-door arrangements;
- emergency-contract profiteering;
- factional appointment;
- private use of public assets;
- protection of allies from accountability.

## 5.1 Corruption Requires Opportunity and Concealment

Corruption pressure rises when there is:

- concentrated discretion;
- scarce access;
- weak audit;
- low compensation or high dependency;
- opaque procurement;
- emergency urgency;
- strong patron networks;
- fear of retaliation;
- fragmented records;
- normalized impunity.

It falls through:

- transparency with privacy safeguards;
- rotation and separation of duties;
- independent review;
- worker protection;
- material security;
- public procurement records;
- accessible appeals;
- credible sanctions;
- institutional culture;
- repair of the conditions that made capture attractive.

## 5.2 Anti-Corruption Must Not Become Surveillance Absolutism

The game must not imply that perfect observation produces justice.

Audit systems must preserve:

- private life;
- protected medical and relationship information;
- anonymous reporting;
- due process;
- proportionality;
- rights of appeal;
- protection against political fishing expeditions.

# 6. Founder Effects and Institutional Maturity

Founders often combine charisma, competence, trust, and informal authority.

This may be useful early and dangerous later.

Institutions should mature through stages:

```text
founder-led improvisation
repeatable practice
formal office and records
professional public service
independent review
succession capacity
institutional renewal or stagnation
```

A founder who refuses to create successors may remain beloved while weakening the future.

A player-founded settlement must eventually face the question:

> Can this place continue without us?

# 7. Machine and Nonhuman Succession

Succession rules must not assume one human body and one legal person.

Examples:

- a machine steward transfers duties but not private memory;
- a swarm polity replaces damaged nodes while preserving pattern continuity;
- a chorus selects a new speaking configuration;
- an archive construct forks into competing interpretations;
- an ecological council changes representative species or seasonal voice;
- a long-lived alien office spans multiple human generations.

The game must separately evaluate:

- continuity of agency;
- continuity of office;
- continuity of credentials;
- continuity of memory;
- continuity of public recognition.

# 8. Player Interaction

Players may:

- train a successor;
- expose or participate in patronage;
- design office limits;
- protect a handover;
- serve as interim operator;
- refuse an illegitimate transfer;
- preserve records;
- rebuild a civil service;
- negotiate a contested succession;
- create a continuity plan;
- support a whistleblower;
- reform procurement;
- choose to relinquish power.

The player must not be able to solve every institutional problem by becoming permanent ruler.

# 9. Failure Continuations

Institutional failure should produce ongoing states, not only a game-over screen.

Examples:

```text
service continues under disputed authority
records split across rival archives
staff obey only safety-critical orders
an interim leader delays elections
public servants form a caretaker administration
citizens create informal parallel services
a corrupt office is removed but expertise collapses
a popular reform succeeds but causes short-term operational failure
```

# 10. Seedworks Representative Scope

The representative build needs only a bounded proof:

- one service office with two authority scopes;
- one named officeholder and one trained deputy;
- one unavailable-leader event;
- one temporary credential transition;
- one public-service handover;
- one conflict-of-interest signal;
- one appeal or review;
- one visible service consequence;
- one Chronicle entry that distinguishes office continuity from personal continuity.

# 11. Acceptance Tests

A succession feature is not ready unless:

1. removing the officeholder does not silently destroy unrelated services;
2. interim authority is scoped and expires or receives review;
3. credentials, records, and unfinished obligations transfer separately;
4. practical expertise can differ from formal authority;
5. at least one staff member can resist or report an improper instruction;
6. corruption evidence does not automatically establish guilt;
7. public audit does not expose protected private cognition or health data;
8. the player can relinquish authority without losing all meaningful play;
9. save/load preserves the transition state;
10. the world can explain who is acting, under what authority, and why.

# 12. Non-Goals

This contract does not require:

- a full parliamentary simulator;
- universal elections;
- one ideal constitutional model;
- every NPC to hold political opinions;
- procedural paperwork for ordinary actions;
- perfect anti-corruption systems;
- player control of all public offices.

## Final Rule

> **A civilization becomes durable when power can leave a person without taking the whole future with it.**
