# Mental Health, Faith, Grief, and Ambiguous Experience Contract

## Purpose

Symtropy may depict mental illness, neurodiversity, grief, altered perception, spiritual experience, doubt, and psychiatric care without treating them as interchangeable forms of “madness.”

The game must preserve dignity, uncertainty, privacy, risk, responsibility, and cultural interpretation.

## Core Principle

> **An unusual experience may be meaningful, distressing, dangerous, sacred, mistaken, or several at once. The game does not need to erase ambiguity in order to protect the person living through it.**

# 1. No Madness Category

The simulation must not use one `madness` variable to explain:

- violence;
- creativity;
- alien contact;
- religious belief;
- criminality;
- unpredictability;
- villainy;
- supernatural truth.

Mental-health state is multidimensional and contextual.

# 2. Relevant Domains

The game may represent:

- depression;
- anxiety;
- panic;
- post-traumatic stress;
- dissociation;
- psychosis;
- mania or hypomania;
- obsessive-compulsive symptoms;
- eating disorders in non-instructional terms;
- cognitive decline;
- developmental and cognitive disability;
- neurodivergence;
- grief;
- moral injury;
- burnout;
- sleep disruption;
- medication effects;
- spiritual crisis;
- culturally mediated experiences.

Representation need not name a diagnosis when the person or culture would not use that framework.

# 3. Person Before Diagnosis

Every character with a mental-health condition requires:

- goals unrelated to symptoms;
- skills and relationships;
- ordinary habits;
- preferences;
- flaws not explained by diagnosis;
- privacy;
- possible joy;
- changing needs;
- agency in care decisions where capacity permits.

A diagnosis does not replace character design.

# 4. Psychosis and Unusual Perception

Psychosis may involve:

- hallucinations;
- delusional beliefs;
- disorganized thought or communication;
- fear;
- altered salience;
- difficulty distinguishing sources;
- periods of insight;
- culturally meaningful interpretation.

Rules:

- psychosis does not automatically cause violence;
- the player's UI must not present every unusual perception as hidden supernatural truth;
- the game may include situations where a fear is partly justified;
- care focuses on safety, distress, sleep, trust, environment, and treatment choices;
- generated dialogue must not caricature incoherence;
- private clinical interpretation does not become public reputation.

IRIS may help compare evidence but cannot diagnose from one conversation or declare an experience unreal with certainty beyond available evidence.

# 5. Mood Disorders

Depression is not poetic sadness or a temporary morale penalty.

It may affect:

- energy;
- pleasure;
- concentration;
- sleep;
- appetite;
- self-worth;
- future expectation;
- social connection;
- task initiation.

Mania or hypomania may involve:

- reduced sleep;
- increased energy;
- confidence;
- rapid thought;
- impulsive projects;
- irritability;
- risk;
- experiences the person values or resists.

The game should not imply that treatment merely removes creativity or that suffering is required for genius.

# 6. Self-Harm and Suicide

The world may acknowledge self-harm, suicidal thought, attempts, and death with strong safeguards.

Rules:

- no detailed method instructions;
- no romanticized or heroic framing;
- no use as punishment for rejecting an NPC;
- no unavoidable surprise scene designed only to shock;
- content warnings and skip options;
- immediate safety, care, and privacy distinctions;
- grief and accountability without assigning one simple cause;
- survivors retain future life and agency;
- IRIS may surface emergency options but must not simulate manipulative panic.

The player cannot be made solely responsible for keeping another character alive through constant attention.

# 7. Trauma and Moral Injury

Trauma may affect:

- attention;
- sleep;
- memory;
- bodily response;
- trust;
- avoidance;
- anger;
- intimacy;
- work;
- sense of safety.

Moral injury may arise from:

- harming others;
- failing to prevent harm;
- betrayal by authority;
- witnessing institutional wrongdoing;
- survival when others died.

Trauma does not excuse all conduct. Accountability and care can coexist.

# 8. Grief

Grief may follow:

- death;
- disappearance;
- failed reconstitution;
- a returned person who is changed;
- loss of home;
- disability;
- migration;
- relationship ending;
- machine shutdown;
- ecological destruction;
- cultural loss;
- worldline divergence.

Grief is not one linear stage sequence.

People may feel:

- sorrow;
- anger;
- relief;
- guilt;
- numbness;
- humor;
- desire;
- practical urgency;
- renewed commitment;
- nothing they expected.

A restored person does not automatically cancel grief experienced during their absence.

# 9. Faith and Spiritual Experience

The game may portray:

- prayer;
- ritual;
- mystical experience;
- visions;
- prophecy claims;
- conversion;
- apostasy;
- religious doubt;
- communal care;
- religious abuse;
- secular meaning-making;
- experiences linked to substances, illness, alien signals, or unknown causes.

The simulation may establish physical evidence but does not secretly mark one metaphysical interpretation as correct.

A vision may be spiritually meaningful even if its sensory mechanism is understood. A medical explanation does not settle theology. A sacred interpretation does not remove the need for safety or consent.

# 10. Religious Communities

Religious communities may provide:

- food;
- shelter;
- care;
- mourning;
- education;
- mediation;
- identity;
- art;
- political organization;
- exclusion;
- coercion;
- status;
- ordinary family habit.

They contain believers, doubters, leaders, workers, opportunists, children, reformers, and people who attend mainly for community.

No religion is represented only by doctrine or villainous leadership.

# 11. Capacity and Supported Decision-Making

Decision capacity is specific to a decision and time.

A person may need support for one decision while remaining capable in others.

Support may include:

- plain-language explanation;
- additional time;
- trusted supporters;
- communication assistance;
- sensory accommodation;
- temporary safety measures;
- advance directives;
- review.

Diagnosis, intoxication, disability, distress, or unusual belief does not automatically remove legal capacity.

When emergency intervention is necessary, authority remains scoped, documented, reviewable, and time limited.

# 12. Care Systems

Care may include:

- peer support;
- psychotherapy or local equivalents;
- medication;
- sleep and environmental stabilization;
- community care;
- spiritual care;
- crisis respite;
- supported housing;
- occupational accommodation;
- addiction treatment;
- trauma-informed medical care;
- long-term relationships.

Treatment has tradeoffs and individual variation.

No single professional, drug, ritual, or ideology works for everyone.

# 13. Psychiatric Coercion

The game may depict involuntary care, restraint, seclusion, forced medication, guardianship, and institutional confinement as serious powers with risk of abuse.

Requirements include:

- immediate and specific safety justification;
- least restrictive available option;
- documentation;
- bodily care;
- communication;
- independent review;
- time limits;
- appeal;
- post-event accountability;
- no use for political inconvenience or social conformity.

The game should show that institutions can both save lives and harm people.

# 14. Violence Risk

Risk assessment considers:

- expressed intent;
- access and capability;
- recent conduct;
- threats;
- acute impairment;
- environmental pressure;
- history with context;
- protective relationships;
- available care;
- uncertainty.

Diagnosis alone is not a violence predictor.

IRIS may warn about observed threats but must not expose a secret “dangerous mentally ill” score.

# 15. Information and Stigma

Mental-health information is private.

Possible harms include:

- employment discrimination;
- loss of custody;
- political discrediting;
- denial of body modification or reconstitution;
- rumor;
- insurance exclusion;
- credibility attacks;
- forced treatment.

Characters may choose public advocacy. That choice does not make every detail public.

# 16. Player Experience

The game may use sensory and interface changes to represent distress or altered perception.

Accessibility requirements:

- reduced distortion modes;
- motion-safe options;
- audio alternatives;
- clear separation of player information from character perception where needed;
- no critical mechanic dependent on enduring disorienting effects;
- content settings for self-harm, psychosis, and traumatic scenes.

# 17. Responsibility

A person with mental illness may harm others. The game can hold them accountable without portraying the diagnosis as the cause of all wrongdoing.

Responses may include:

- safety restrictions;
- treatment;
- restitution;
- legal process;
- relationship boundaries;
- institutional review;
- recognition of impaired capacity where supported by evidence.

Care does not require victims to resume contact.

# 18. Ambiguous Contact and Alien Experience

Alien or machine cognition may produce experiences humans misclassify as pathology, religion, signal, or manipulation.

The game preserves hypotheses such as:

- sensory difference;
- communication;
- illness;
- environmental response;
- ritual;
- distributed cognition;
- hostile interference;
- translation error.

Uncertainty does not justify unrestricted experimentation or dismissal.

# 19. Authoring Requirements

A mental-health or faith storyline must specify:

- the person's ordinary life;
- their own interpretation;
- competing interpretations;
- privacy;
- material and social context;
- risks without stereotypes;
- care and refusal options;
- relationship boundaries;
- continuity after the crisis;
- content and accessibility controls.

# 20. Acceptance Failures

This contract fails if:

- mental illness becomes a violence class;
- unusual perception always reveals supernatural truth;
- diagnosis becomes public automatically;
- one medication or ritual is universally correct;
- treatment removes personhood or creativity by design;
- self-harm is romanticized or used to punish rejection;
- the player becomes solely responsible for another person's survival;
- involuntary care lacks review and limits;
- grief ends because restoration occurred;
- faith is treated only as wisdom or fraud;
- IRIS diagnoses or invalidates experience without evidence;
- accessibility requires exposure to disorienting effects.

## Closing Principle

> **Symtropy can be honest about minds in pain without turning suffering into mystery, danger into identity, or ambiguity into an excuse to stop caring.**
