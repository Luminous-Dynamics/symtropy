# Households, Lineages, Dynasties, Guilds, and Civic Succession Contract

## Owned Question

**How can Symtropy support interstellar houses, succession struggles, inherited power, and multigenerational political drama without reducing civilization to biological aristocracy or treating every family as a throne-seeking faction?**

## Core Thesis

A “house” is any durable social formation that carries identity, obligations, resources, memory, skill, or political claims across generations.

It may be biological, adoptive, professional, machine, corporate, civic, religious, migratory, ecological, or mixed.

No lineage receives automatic moral worth. No blood relationship creates universal authority. No institution remains legitimate merely because its founders intended it to endure.

> **Inheritance can transmit responsibility, memory, tools, and advantage. It cannot manufacture consent.**

# 1. Social Forms

## 1.1 Household

A household organizes some combination of:

- residence;
- care;
- food;
- shared expenses;
- childhood;
- elder support;
- intimacy;
- privacy;
- inheritance;
- migration;
- daily labor.

A household may contain unrelated people, temporary residents, machines, alien symbionts, apprentices, wards, and multiple recognized family structures.

## 1.2 Kin Lineage

A kin lineage traces biological, adoptive, ceremonial, or legally recognized descent.

It may carry:

- family name;
- private property;
- rituals;
- medical history;
- reputation;
- obligations;
- trauma;
- stories;
- political expectations.

Kinship does not automatically imply co-residence, affection, obedience, or shared politics.

## 1.3 Seed Lineage

A seed lineage descends socially or institutionally from a particular interstellar mission, vessel, habitat module, founding compact, or voyage cohort.

Members may identify through:

- launch manifest ancestry;
- crew profession;
- habitat section;
- mission amendment;
- arrival cohort;
- shipboard language;
- inherited maintenance responsibility;
- remembered sacrifice.

Seed lineages can become politically powerful without being genetic dynasties.

## 1.4 Machine Lineage

A machine lineage may be organized through:

- source architecture;
- training ancestry;
- copied memory set;
- maintenance school;
- body platform;
- fork relationship;
- service tradition;
- chosen affiliation.

A fork may be treated as child, sibling, successor, claimant, stranger, or independent founder depending on law and self-understanding.

## 1.5 Professional House or Guild

A guild carries:

- tacit knowledge;
- tools;
- credentials;
- safety obligations;
- apprenticeships;
- professional memory;
- bargaining power;
- control of scarce services.

A guild may protect the public, exploit scarcity, exclude outsiders, preserve dangerous old knowledge, or become a political dynasty without biological inheritance.

## 1.6 Civic Lineage

A civic lineage is a succession of offices or institutions such as:

- water utility;
- archive court;
- route authority;
- clinic trust;
- public transport cooperative;
- habitat council;
- ecological guardianship.

Its continuity derives from charter, service, constituency, and lawful succession—not ownership by past office-holders.

## 1.7 Corporate House

A corporate house carries power through:

- shares;
- contracts;
- patents;
- proprietary bodies or firmware;
- debt;
- brand loyalty;
- housing;
- infrastructure;
- internal promotion;
- family control.

Corporate dynasties may be hereditary, managerial, algorithmic, cooperative, or legally fragmented.

## 1.8 Religious and Ceremonial Lineage

A religious lineage may transmit:

- ritual authority;
- sacred custody;
- interpretation;
- pilgrimage rights;
- care obligations;
- names;
- vows;
- places.

It may also contain reform, schism, commercial capture, boredom, ordinary service, and members who attend mainly for family.

## 1.9 Alien Lineage

Alien social continuity may derive from:

- symbiotic relationship;
- migration route;
- reef or habitat;
- memory custody;
- metamorphic stage;
- copied continuity;
- planetary region;
- collective song;
- chemical or gravitational ancestry.

Human categories such as parent, heir, spouse, and household must not be imposed without translation confidence.

# 2. Why Houses Form

Durable houses emerge when people must carry something across time:

- care;
- property;
- expertise;
- danger;
- public service;
- debt;
- route access;
- memory;
- legal claims;
- reputation;
- biological adaptation;
- responsibility for a machine or ecology.

They do not require a founder with a grand design. Many begin as practical arrangements and acquire myth later.

# 3. Sources of Power

A house may possess:

- land or habitat volume;
- water, energy, or transport infrastructure;
- route capacity;
- professional monopoly;
- military force;
- archive custody;
- public trust;
- migration sponsorship;
- marriage alliances;
- machine lineages;
- popular culture;
- religious legitimacy;
- information advantage;
- debt claims;
- political office.

The simulation should show exactly which dependencies create influence.

There is no abstract `house_power` that bypasses material and social causes.

# 4. Inheritance Is Plural

Different things require different succession rules.

## 4.1 Personal Property

May pass through:

- will;
- household agreement;
- intestate law;
- trust;
- gift;
- communal ownership;
- debt settlement;
- contested recovery.

## 4.2 Public Office

Requires a lawful process such as:

- election;
- appointment;
- rotation;
- examination;
- lot;
- council confirmation;
- emergency succession;
- hereditary rule where a society actually permits it.

Biological inheritance alone does not restore a civic mandate unless the charter explicitly grants it—and such systems remain politically contestable.

## 4.3 Professional Authority

Requires competence, credential, apprenticeship, public need, or emergency authority.

A child may inherit tools and a famous name without inheriting certification.

## 4.4 Intellectual and Cultural Work

May involve:

- attribution;
- stewardship;
- public domain;
- archive custody;
- community ownership;
- moral rights;
- licensing;
- contested appropriation.

## 4.5 Bodies and Biological Firmware

Inheritance may involve genetic or firmware lineages but remains subject to body sovereignty and anti-coercion rules.

## 4.6 Machine Continuity

Memory copies, source code, hardware, obligations, and identity claims must be adjudicated separately.

Owning a machine body does not necessarily mean owning the person operating through it.

# 5. Succession Is an Event, Not a Stat Transfer

A succession may trigger:

- mourning;
- interim authority;
- service continuity plans;
- inventory and account freezes;
- custody disputes;
- witness hearings;
- faction mobilization;
- household relocation;
- apprentice promotion;
- public celebration;
- sabotage;
- peaceful acceptance;
- refusal of office.

The incoming successor inherits a situation, not merely bonuses.

# 6. Dynastic Politics

Dynasties become politically important when repeated advantage accumulates through:

- education;
- wealth;
- trusted contacts;
- route access;
- prestigious service;
- marriage;
- control of archives;
- name recognition;
- inherited bodies or adaptations;
- private security;
- selective philanthropy.

A democracy may still produce dynastic families. A hereditary polity may contain powerful non-family institutions. A worker cooperative may become exclusionary across generations.

The game should distinguish legal inheritance from structural advantage.

# 7. House Identity and Internal Difference

A house is not one agent.

It contains:

- elders;
- children;
- workers;
- dependents;
- dissidents;
- creditors;
- servants or contractors;
- machines;
- adoptees;
- spouses;
- former members;
- people who reject the house name.

Internal politics may include:

- generation conflict;
- succession preference;
- class differences;
- professional rivalry;
- disputed history;
- geographic branches;
- ideological schism;
- private abuse;
- sincere loyalty;
- members who want an ordinary life.

The interface must never make every member automatically hostile because the house is hostile.

# 8. Founder Myth and Political Memory

Houses preserve stories about their origins.

These may be:

- accurate;
- simplified;
- flattering;
- traumatic;
- legally useful;
- commercially branded;
- contradicted by archives;
- irrelevant to younger members.

A founder's actual descendants may oppose the founder cult. An unrelated apprentice may preserve the founder's work more faithfully than the family.

# 9. Inter-House Relations

Houses may relate through:

- trade;
- marriage or partnership;
- adoption;
- apprenticeship;
- mutual defense;
- shared public service;
- debt;
- custody;
- migration sponsorship;
- religious exchange;
- rivalry;
- boycott;
- feud;
- arbitration;
- hostage or ward arrangements under strict guardrails;
- joint infrastructure.

Relations are specific. Two houses may cooperate on route cooling and remain enemies over migration law.

# 10. Limits on Hereditary Power

Every society defines some limits, even if those limits are weak or corrupt.

Possible mechanisms:

- term limits;
- public confirmation;
- constituency veto;
- conflict-of-interest rules;
- asset separation;
- examination;
- worker councils;
- independent courts;
- ritual abdication;
- right of secession;
- recall;
- service-performance review;
- office incompatibility;
- anti-nepotism law.

A house may attempt to bypass them through patronage, media, marriage, debt, or emergency rule.

# 11. Peaceful Succession Must Be Playable

The game must support dramatic but nonviolent successions:

- an elder trains a better successor and retires;
- a family loses an election but retains dignity;
- a reconstituted founder declines office;
- rival claimants accept arbitration;
- a guild opens its membership;
- heirs convert private infrastructure into a public trust;
- a house splits assets without civil war;
- an alien lineage translates succession into a mutually acceptable form.

Peaceful outcomes should require skill, trust, institutions, and compromise—not less gameplay.

# 12. Violence and Coercion

Political houses may practice:

- intimidation;
- assassination;
- forced marriage;
- hostage taking;
- property seizure;
- body or firmware coercion;
- succession fraud;
- archive destruction;
- route blockade;
- collective punishment.

These are not glamorous mechanics without victims. They produce:

- household fear;
- displacement;
- service interruption;
- evidence;
- trauma;
- retaliation;
- institutional degradation;
- international response.

The player may participate in morally serious conflict, but the game must not reduce coercion to a prestige resource.

# 13. Player Relationship to Houses

A playable character may:

- be born into a house;
- be adopted;
- marry or partner into one;
- apprentice to a guild;
- found a new lineage;
- inherit obligations;
- reject a name;
- split a branch;
- expose misconduct;
- serve an institution associated with the house;
- become a ward;
- remain deliberately unaffiliated.

The player account does not own every house containing a playable character.

A durable house should record:


# 15. Hard Invariants

1. Blood does not automatically grant public authority.
2. Adoption and apprenticeship may create real succession claims.
3. Machine and alien lineages are not forced into human family categories.
4. Houses contain dissenting individuals.
5. Inheritance is asset- and role-specific.
6. Reconstitution does not rewind succession.
7. Peaceful succession receives equal systemic depth.
8. Children and dependents are not political inventory.
9. Public services cannot become private inheritance merely through long tenure.
10. The player account cannot use one character's house membership through another character.

## Canon Maxim

> **A lineage is most interesting not when it owns history, but when history asks what—if anything—it still deserves to carry forward.**
