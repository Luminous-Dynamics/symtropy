# Profession Simulation, Embodied Mastery, and Public Responsibility Contract

## Purpose

This contract defines professions as sustained practices rather than classes, task lists, or numerical bonuses.

It deepens the existing profession-loop vision by adding the scales at which real work becomes socially and emotionally meaningful: preparation, shifts, crews, projects, careers, institutions, bodily cost, livelihood, teaching, and unfinished responsibility.

## Core Principle

> **A profession changes what the player can notice, judge, accomplish, explain, and remain accountable for after the visible task is over.**

# 1. Profession Is Not Class

A profession does not define a permanent combat role or lock the player into one identity.

It is a relationship among:

```text
perception
practice
tools
materials
body
crew
institution
law
economy
public trust
history
```

Players may learn several professions, but deep practice requires time, relationships, equipment, and responsibility.

# 2. Ten Required Layers

Every deep profession must define:

1. sensory vocabulary;
2. tactile or social execution;
3. diagnostic uncertainty;
4. tools and material dependencies;
5. safety and bodily cost;
6. crew and institutional context;
7. shift and project rhythm;
8. economic life;
9. public responsibility;
10. teaching, succession, and legacy.

A profession packet that omits these layers is a supporting activity, not a deep profession.

# 3. Distinct Professional Tempo

Professions must not expose the same reskinned `scan → diagnose → perform → record` rhythm.

Examples:

- a medic works against biological time and consent boundaries;
- a machinist works toward tolerance and repeatability;
- a mediator works through status, silence, willingness, and procedural legitimacy;
- a convoy planner acts before the danger is visible;
- a performer builds attention, timing, and collective emotion;
- a sanitation worker reads the aftermath others ignore;
- a witness protects evidence against contamination and political pressure.

The shared causal grammar may remain hidden. Player-facing tempo must differ.

# 4. Scales of Practice

## Moment

A skilled physical, perceptual, analytic, or social action.

## Task

One bounded repair, treatment, inspection, service, performance, delivery, hearing, or investigation.

## Shift

A period containing preparation, workload, breaks, handovers, shortages, interruptions, relationships, and unfinished work.

## Project

A multi-shift effort such as an overhaul, festival, court inquiry, epidemic response, convoy season, harvest, construction, or restoration.

## Career

The accumulation of style, reputation, injury, relationships, credentials, obligations, apprentices, rivals, and remembered outcomes.

Progression must operate at all five scales.

# 5. Preparation, Support, and Cleanup

The game should represent work surrounding the spectacular moment:

- reviewing history;
- securing authority;
- gathering and checking tools;
- preparing people and spaces;
- isolating hazards;
- arranging support;
- performing the task;
- testing under realistic conditions;
- cleaning and restoring the site;
- documenting uncertainty;
- handing work to others;
- returning for follow-up.

These steps become playable when they involve judgment, scarcity, delegation, or consequence. They must not become repetitive maintenance chores without decisions.

# 6. Tacit Knowledge

Some expertise is embodied and difficult to reduce to explicit instructions.

The game may represent tacit knowledge through:

- better perception of subtle cues;
- steadier control;
- recognition before full diagnosis;
- contextual memory;
- reliable intuition with uncertainty;
- fewer false positives;
- ability to teach through demonstration.

IRIS may reveal evidence and patterns. It must not erase the value of lived practice or claim certainty the sensors do not support.

# 7. Tools Are Working Histories

Tools differ through:

- calibration;
- wear;
- ergonomics;
- repair history;
- contamination;
- manufacturer and standards;
- firmware;
- local modifications;
- compatibility;
- ownership and access rights.

Tools are not colored loot tiers.

A familiar repaired tool may be preferable to a superior locked instrument. A precise instrument may still produce false confidence when calibration or assumptions are wrong.

# 8. Crews and Institutions

No profession exists only as an isolated skill.

Practice is shaped by:

- coworkers;
- supervisors;
- dispatch;
- support staff;
- unions and guilds;
- regulators;
- clients and patients;
- vendors;
- insurers;
- households;
- informal networks;
- public expectations.

The same profession should feel different inside a cooperative, public service, corporation, military unit, illegal clinic, household, religious institution, or expedition.

# 9. Workplace Culture

Workplaces may develop:

- slang;
- jokes;
- rituals;
- unofficial shortcuts;
- status markers;
- taboos;
- songs;
- legends;
- rivalries;
- initiation practices;
- definitions of good work;
- scapegoats;
- break-room politics.

Culture can protect safety, transmit tacit skill, conceal abuse, resist management, or create exclusion.

# 10. Safety and Bodily Cost

Professions may create:

- fatigue;
- repetitive-strain injury;
- hearing or vision damage;
- chemical or radiation exposure;
- chronic pain;
- sleep disruption;
- respiratory harm;
- trauma;
- burnout;
- gravity or pressure adaptation.

The design must make protective equipment, staffing, rest, accommodation, and labor politics meaningful.

It must not make self-destruction the optimal mastery path.

# 11. Livelihood and Ownership

Professional life includes:

- wages;
- tips;
- contracts;
- retainers;
- cooperative shares;
- unpaid care;
- seasonal demand;
- equipment and credential costs;
- insurance;
- liability;
- debt;
- prestige;
- household obligations.

Income and social value are separate. Essential work may be underpaid. Prestigious work may be precarious.

# 12. Credentials and Authority

Credentials represent some combination of:

- demonstrated competence;
- public responsibility;
- insurance;
- legal authority;
- institutional trust;
- monopoly;
- historical scandal;
- political exclusion.

Players may sometimes be capable but unauthorized.

Possible choices include:

- wait;
- obtain supervised authority;
- act under emergency rules;
- accept liability;
- document an informal intervention;
- challenge a corrupt credential system.

Credentials must not become arbitrary level gates.

# 13. Professional Judgment

High mastery increasingly concerns conflicts without one perfect technical answer.

Examples:

- certify limited structural use or displace residents;
- preserve privacy or warn a population;
- keep a venue open under mitigation or close it and eliminate income;
- repair quickly with a temporary method or wait for durable material;
- treat one urgent patient or several deteriorating ones;
- publish evidence that may be misunderstood;
- follow procedure or adapt to an unprecedented body or environment.

Mastery is partly knowing when a technical problem has become civic, ethical, or political.

# 14. Style and Practice Identity

Progression should create differentiated masters.

A player may become known for:

- reversible interventions;
- speed under crisis;
- low-resource improvisation;
- historical systems;
- careful documentation;
- difficult clients;
- worker advocacy;
- dangerous shortcuts;
- teaching;
- institutional reform.

No universal optimal style should erase these differences.

# 15. Teaching and Succession

Profession continuity requires:

- demonstration;
- supervised practice;
- feedback;
- access to tools and cases;
- error review;
- tacit transmission;
- documentation;
- credential or community recognition;
- opportunities to refuse unsafe orders.

An archive can preserve knowledge without preserving competence.

A master who refuses to teach can create a civilization-scale vulnerability.

# 16. Failure and Repair

Professional failure may involve:

- misdiagnosis;
- poor execution;
- rushed preparation;
- hidden material defect;
- bad handover;
- institutional pressure;
- fatigue;
- corruption;
- inadequate support;
- unprecedented conditions;
- unavoidable loss.

The game must distinguish negligence, uncertainty, system failure, and bad luck.

Failure should support investigation, learning, accountability, repair, and sometimes lasting loss.

# 17. Co-op

Co-op professional play should create complementary perception and authority rather than rigid classes.

Players may divide:

- execution;
- diagnosis;
- logistics;
- witness and documentation;
- safety;
- communication;
- public procedure.

The design should reward clear handovers and cross-profession trust without requiring one fixed party composition.

# 18. IRIS Boundary

IRIS may:

- retrieve records;
- explain instrument readings;
- preserve checklists;
- surface uncertainty;
- compare procedures;
- record handovers;
- warn about known interactions;
- support accessibility.

IRIS may not:

- silently perform judgment for the player;
- certify work without authority;
- claim tacit expertise from generic data;
- hide uncertainty to create a cleaner interface;
- expose private professional records;
- make a worker morally responsible for institution-level failures without evidence.

# 19. Acceptance Tests

A deep profession must demonstrate:

- at least three distinct sensory or interpretive cues;
- meaningful preparation and aftermath;
- one shift-level resource or relationship problem;
- one project-level dependency;
- multiple credible practice styles;
- tool condition and compatibility;
- a safety or bodily-cost boundary;
- economic and institutional context;
- a handover that can succeed or fail;
- a decision where technical correctness is insufficient;
- teaching or succession;
- a persistent reputation grounded in actual work.

## Canonical Summary

Professions are not menus of permitted actions.

They are lived disciplines through which people notice the world, take responsibility for changing it, and leave work that others must inherit.
