# War Atrocity, Detention, Displacement, and Postwar Accountability Contract

## Purpose

Symtropy may depict war without sanitizing the systems that make civilian harm possible.

Maturity does not require graphic spectacle. It requires showing how orders, logistics, fear, bureaucracy, prejudice, infrastructure, and impunity turn violence into durable history.

## Core Principle

> **War does not suspend causality, personhood, custody, evidence, or responsibility. It makes their preservation harder and more urgent.**

# 1. Civilian Status

Civilians remain persons with:

- homes;
- work;
- care obligations;
- medical needs;
- documents;
- property and use rights;
- cultural and religious sites;
- movement constraints;
- private information;
- political opinions that do not erase protection.

The game must not turn civilian populations into one casualty number.

Named people and households remain traceable under simulation level of detail.

# 2. Civilian Harm

Civilian harm may result from:

- direct attack;
- indiscriminate weapons;
- infrastructure destruction;
- siege;
- starvation;
- disease;
- displacement;
- environmental contamination;
- detention;
- forced labor;
- sexual violence;
- unexploded weapons;
- loss of care systems;
- retaliation;
- misinformation;
- blocked rescue;
- postwar neglect.

The runtime distinguishes intent, knowledge, foreseeability, negligence, accident, command pressure, and system failure.

A lawful target does not erase all consequences of the chosen method.

# 3. Protected and Dual-Use Infrastructure

Hospitals, shelters, water systems, life support, archives, schools, religious sites, evacuation routes, and rescue channels receive explicit status.

Some may also support military activity.

The game must represent:

- verification;
- proportionality and alternative means under local rules;
- warnings;
- misuse by armed groups;
- civilian dependency;
- evidence after attack;
- responsibility for restoration.

“Dual use” is not a universal permission to destroy essential civilian systems.

# 4. Siege and Starvation

Siege is physical control over routes and resources.

It may affect:

- food;
- water;
- medicine;
- air and power;
- fuel;
- sanitation;
- evacuation;
- communication;
- body-firmware and machine maintenance;
- agricultural cycles.

Starvation policy is not represented as a clean pressure modifier.

It produces:

- unequal household choices;
- black markets;
- disease;
- institutional collapse;
- coercion;
- child and elder risk;
- military desertion;
- long-term health effects;
- evidence and accountability.

# 5. Displacement

Displacement requires physical movement and destination capacity.

People carry:

- dependents;
- medicine;
- assistive devices;
- animals or machine companions;
- identity records;
- tools;
- cultural objects;
- claims to home;
- grief and uncertainty.

The game distinguishes:

- evacuation;
- flight;
- forced transfer;
- deportation;
- expulsion;
- temporary shelter;
- long-term exile;
- return;
- local integration;
- contested property restoration.

A ceasefire does not teleport displaced people home.

# 6. Detention

Detention requires:

- lawful or claimed authority;
- identity and custody record;
- reason;
- review;
- humane conditions;
- medical care;
- communication;
- complaint channels;
- release, transfer, or adjudication path.

The game may depict unlawful detention, disappearance, torture, coercive interrogation, and degrading conditions, but not as entertainment minigames.

Player mechanics must not reward torture as a uniquely effective information source.

Information obtained under coercion is unreliable and creates legal, moral, and strategic consequences.

# 7. Prisoners and Surrender

Surrendering or incapacitated combatants retain protection.

Rules include:

- no execution for convenience;
- disarmament and search with dignity;
- medical triage based on need;
- custody records;
- protection from revenge;
- exchange or release processes;
- accountability for prior crimes through evidence and law rather than immediate retaliation.

A prisoner may remain dangerous. Safety restrictions do not erase personhood.

# 8. Sexual Violence

Sexual violence may be referenced as a real wartime harm.

Representation rules:

- no graphic depiction;
- no player-performed mechanics;
- no use as routine faction flavor;
- no assumption that every survivor responds similarly;
- preserve privacy and medical autonomy;
- evidence and reporting remain voluntary where possible;
- children are protected from sexualized content entirely;
- survivor life continues beyond the event;
- command and institutional responsibility may be investigated.

The game may show aftermath, care, testimony, denial, cover-up, and accountability with restraint.

# 9. Unlawful Orders and Refusal

Combatants may receive orders involving:

- attack on protected sites;
- collective punishment;
- execution;
- forced displacement;
- destruction of food or water;
- falsification of records;
- abandonment of civilians;
- prohibited weapons;
- concealment of casualties.

NPCs respond according to knowledge, fear, values, training, discipline, relationships, and available alternatives.

Refusal may carry risk. Compliance does not remove responsibility automatically.

The game should represent:

- protest;
- delay;
- clarification requests;
- partial compliance;
- rescue of intended victims;
- whistleblowing;
- desertion;
- later confession;
- cover-up.

# 10. Command Responsibility

Responsibility can exist at multiple levels:

- direct perpetrator;
- immediate commander;
- senior command;
- political leadership;
- intelligence or targeting staff;
- logistics personnel;
- corporate supplier;
- detention administrator;
- institution that ignored known patterns.

The game distinguishes what each actor knew, should have known, could control, and did afterward.

Removing one villain does not erase the systems that enabled the harm.

# 11. Evidence

War evidence may include:

- sensor records;
- Field Deck testimony;
- orders;
- route logs;
- munitions and damage patterns;
- bodies and medical records;
- detention registers;
- satellite or orbital observations;
- witness testimony;
- intercepted messages;
- mass-grave or ruin evidence;
- missing records;
- deliberate falsification.

Evidence requires custody, privacy, interpretation, and safety for witnesses.

IRIS may help reconstruct events but must preserve uncertainty and cannot replace a court or public process.

# 12. Veterans

Veterans are not one archetype.

They may be:

- proud;
- ashamed;
- injured;
- politically active;
- silent;
- celebrated;
- abandoned;
- guilty of wrongdoing;
- whistleblowers;
- survivors of abuse;
- perpetrators and victims in different contexts;
- ordinary people trying to work.

Trauma does not automatically make veterans violent or noble.

Veterans may resist accountability. They may also lead it.

# 13. Postwar Accountability

Possible processes include:

- local courts;
- military justice;
- truth commissions;
- public inquiries;
- archive reconstruction;
- reparations;
- property return;
- missing-person identification;
- institutional vetting;
- amnesty under limited conditions;
- international or inter-polity tribunals;
- restorative processes chosen by affected people;
- memorial and educational work.

No mechanism resolves every need.

Peace may require coexistence before agreement about history.

# 14. Reconstruction

Reconstruction includes:

- housing;
- utilities;
- land and route clearance;
- care systems;
- schools;
- archives;
- livelihoods;
- environmental repair;
- institution rebuilding;
- veteran reintegration;
- survivor privacy;
- return and local integration;
- cultural restoration.

Contracts, aid, and land allocation may reproduce wartime inequality.

The player should be able to see who performs reconstruction labor and who profits.

# 15. Children and Long Consequence

Children may experience:

- displacement;
- interrupted education;
- family separation;
- injury;
- hunger;
- lost language or home;
- unexploded hazards;
- adult secrecy;
- inherited political identity.

The game avoids graphic harm to children and never uses it as cheap motivation.

Long-term systems should show education, care, reunion, missing-person searches, and adult lives shaped but not wholly defined by war.

# 16. Player Conduct

Players may commit or enable serious harm in systems that permit it.

The game should respond through:

- witnesses;
- evidence;
- faction and institutional action;
- refusal by allies;
- legal process;
- retaliation;
- reputation by domain;
- loss of authority;
- personal memory;
- worldline history.

Consequences must not become a simplistic morality meter.

Servers may restrict or prohibit conduct that creates harassment or nonconsensual player experience even when analogous NPC actions exist in fiction.

# 17. Presentation Boundaries

Mature war content should emphasize:

- choices before harm;
- physical aftermath;
- survivor and worker perspectives;
- bureaucracy;
- missing people;
- repair;
- evidence;
- unresolved responsibility;
- ordinary life continuing.

Avoid:

- gore as proof of seriousness;
- endless civilian misery;
- heroic framing of every refusal;
- atrocity as faction decoration;
- sexual violence as villain shorthand;
- immediate catharsis through execution;
- cynical claims that everyone is equally guilty;
- treating accountability as naïve.

# 18. Acceptance Failures

This contract fails if:

- civilian harm is only a number;
- sieges do not affect physical flows;
- displacement teleports populations;
- torture produces reliable information bonuses;
- surrender creates disposable targets;
- unlawful orders have no refusal path;
- command responsibility is ignored;
- sexual violence is graphic or routine;
- war ends and all social consequences reset;
- one trial repairs an institution;
- veterans are reduced to heroes, monsters, or trauma machines;
- Field Deck reconstruction is treated as infallible truth.

## Closing Principle

> **The game is unafraid to depict war when it remains interested in the people, records, labor, bodies, and institutions that violence attempts to reduce to collateral.**
