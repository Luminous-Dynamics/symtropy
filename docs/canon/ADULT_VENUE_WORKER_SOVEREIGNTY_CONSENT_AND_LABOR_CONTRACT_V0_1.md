# Adult Venue Worker Sovereignty, Consent, and Labor Contract

## Purpose

This contract governs adult-only workplaces and services in pleasure cities.

It permits mature, erotic, intimate, commercial, ritual, and performance-based adult content while preventing the simulation from converting workers into decorative resources, purchasable relationships, or proof that the setting is “edgy.”

## Core Principle

> **An adult venue is legitimate only while every worker and participant retains practical power to define, refuse, stop, document, and leave the activity.**

# 1. Scope

The contract applies to:

- erotic dance and performance;
- consensual sexual services;
- adult companionship and intimacy work;
- adult bathhouses and private social venues;
- fetish, roleplay, body-art, and sensory-performance venues;
- adult media production and distribution;
- ritualized adult intimacy;
- independent, cooperative, public, corporate, informal, and criminalized workplaces;
- support roles whose labor is necessary to operate these venues.

It also applies where an ostensibly nonsexual venue pressures workers to provide intimacy, flirtation, or access beyond their contracts.

# 2. Authoritative Adulthood and Capacity

Every participant must be authoritatively verified as an adult under the applicable polity and species/life-stage framework.

The game must not infer adulthood from appearance, body form, role, memory age, reconstitution age, machine embodiment, or alien translation convenience.

Participation requires current capacity. Capacity may be affected by:

- intoxication;
- sedation;
- cognitive crisis;
- coercive threat;
- dependence on an authority holder;
- incomplete translation;
- medical instability;
- interrupted identity or source-chain verification.

Uncertainty fails closed for the activity, not against the person’s dignity or freedom of movement.

# 3. Consent Is Specific and Operational

Consent state must specify:

```text
participants
activity boundary
location
privacy scope
recording permissions
payment or exchange terms
protective requirements
communication method
stop signals
expiry or review point
```

Consent to one service, performance, client, recording, venue, or relationship does not imply consent to another.

A person may withdraw consent at any point. Withdrawal may create contractual consequences only where those consequences do not punish the refusal itself or trap the person in further participation.

# 4. Worker Sovereignty

Workers must have practical control over:

- which clients or audiences they accept;
- which services or performances they offer;
- schedule and shift limits;
- bodily and sensory boundaries;
- protective equipment and health precautions;
- whether and how they are recorded;
- chosen names and public identity;
- access to earnings and tips;
- access to transport, medicine, documents, charging, body maintenance, and communication;
- complaint, appeal, and representation.

A venue is exploitative when formal consent exists but practical refusal would cost essential housing, air, medicine, identity, immigration status, body firmware, or source-chain continuity.

# 5. Employment Models

Supported models include:

## Independent Practice

High autonomy and direct income, with risks involving isolation, platform dependence, private security, unstable demand, and administrative burden.

## Worker Cooperative

Shared ownership, collective safety, and democratic policy, with possible membership exclusion, internal status hierarchy, and conflict over commercial pressure.

## Corporate Venue

Capital, insurance, polished infrastructure, and predictable demand, with risks involving surveillance, quotas, housing dependency, brand control, and contractual coercion.

## Public or Licensed Utility

Transparent standards and public services, with risks involving stigma, bureaucracy, political interference, and paternalistic surveillance.

## Informal or Criminalized Work

May preserve autonomy where formal institutions exclude workers, but may also produce extortion, unsafe conditions, document control, and weak appeal.

No ownership model is automatically safe or exploitative.

# 6. Support Labor Is Visible

Adult venues require and must account for:

- reception and scheduling;
- sanitation and laundry;
- security and de-escalation;
- health services;
- consent monitoring where chosen;
- transport;
- accounting and custody;
- maintenance;
- translation and accessibility support;
- food, hydration, and rest;
- legal and worker representation.

The simulation fails if it renders intimate labor while erasing the people who clean, protect, transport, schedule, or care for the workers.

# 7. Privacy and Identity

Workers and clients may use separate public, professional, legal, household, and archive identities where law permits.

Protected information may include:

- legal name;
- body and health information;
- client history;
- service history;
- earnings;
- immigration or residency status;
- relationship history;
- source-chain and reconstitution state;
- private IRIS records;
- worker exclusion lists.

Public licensing does not authorize public disclosure of private participation.

IRIS may preserve private boundaries and warn its own user. It may not expose other people’s records or infer undisclosed work from location traces.

# 8. Payment, Debt, and Economic Coercion

Valid payment requires:

- disclosed price or compensation method;
- worker control over accepted terms;
- itemized fees;
- accessible earnings;
- no hidden debt assignment;
- no confiscation of tips or identity records;
- no prohibited collateral involving bodies, medicine, life support, or source chains.

The simulation must represent unpaid preparation, cleanup, cancellations, platform fees, house fees, security costs, and administrative work.

Debt created by travel, housing, licensing, clothing, body maintenance, or recruitment must be visible and contestable. Debt cannot convert future consent into an asset.

# 9. Power Asymmetry

Elevated review applies where one participant controls another’s:

- job;
- housing;
- license;
- immigration status;
- medical access;
- body firmware;
- criminal case;
- custody or detention;
- professional evaluation;
- source-chain verification.

The game must support workers refusing powerful clients without treating that refusal as a failed persuasion encounter.

# 10. Safety Without Policing by Default

Safety may be provided through:

- worker-controlled exclusion lists;
- de-escalation staff;
- peer response;
- venue security;
- transport;
- private panic signals;
- medical support;
- community accountability;
- formal law enforcement where trusted and requested.

A safety model may itself become coercive through surveillance, discriminatory profiling, corruption, or retaliation.

# 11. Trafficking and Coercion Indicators

Indicators may include:

- confiscated documents;
- controlled transport;
- inaccessible earnings;
- threats to family or source chain;
- medication or firmware control;
- mandatory housing;
- unexplained debt;
- inability to refuse clients;
- scripted testimony under supervision;
- inconsistent custody records.

Indicators create grounds for inquiry and support, not automatic guilt or forced “rescue.”

Intervention must preserve the affected person’s agency and avoid making them homeless, undocumented, medically unsupported, or criminalized.

# 12. Health and Reproductive Autonomy

Workers retain the same rights to:

- reproductive healthcare;
- contraception;
- pregnancy care;
- abortion where available;
- infection prevention and treatment;
- disability accommodation;
- body modification;
- refusal of testing or treatment within lawful safety boundaries;
- confidential care.

Health programs may not become de facto public registries of adult workers.

# 13. Leaving, Changing Work, and Retirement

Meaningful exit may require:

- savings or income transition;
- transferable credentials;
- housing;
- transport;
- healthcare;
- identity protection;
- legal support;
- education;
- repair of body or machine dependencies;
- social support.

Leaving must not require the worker to denounce the profession, accept a rescue narrative, or become romantically attached to the player.

Some workers enjoy the work and remain voluntarily. That outcome is not treated as failure.

# 14. Player Relationship Boundary

The player may:

- work in, own, regulate, patronize, protect, investigate, oppose, or reform adult venues;
- support workers’ organizing and infrastructure;
- form adult relationships through separate, ongoing, mutual choices.

The player may not:

- acquire intimacy as a business perk;
- use ownership or employment authority as consent;
- receive sex or romance for completing a rescue;
- use generated dialogue to override refusal;
- convert another person’s participation into a collectible record.

# 15. Institutional Failure Modes

Possible failures include:

- respected cooperative leadership concealing harassment;
- public licensing becoming a registry for political repression;
- corporate safety technology becoming worker surveillance;
- anti-trafficking enforcement criminalizing migrants;
- worker exclusion lists being sold;
- landlords using adult-work stigma to evict tenants;
- venue owners externalizing health and transport costs;
- reform movements speaking over workers.

# 16. Acceptance Tests

The design must prove that:

- a worker can refuse a powerful client without losing authoritative consent state;
- withdrawing consent stops the relevant activity;
- venue profitability includes support labor and cleanup;
- private work identity does not leak through ordinary telemetry;
- exit support can succeed without forcing a moral conversion;
- a worker cooperative can still develop internal exploitation;
- an apparently legal venue can be investigated through causal evidence;
- an intervention can worsen conditions if services are removed without replacement;
- an adult worker can remain satisfied with the profession;
- the player cannot purchase or earn a person.

## Canonical Summary

Adult venues may be joyful, artistic, commercial, sacred, awkward, exploitative, liberating, corrupt, ordinary, or contradictory.

Their legitimacy depends on the practical sovereignty of the people who work and participate in them.
