# Domestic Coercive Control, Abuse, Escape, and Survivor Agency Contract

## Purpose

Domestic abuse in Symtropy is not reduced to visible assault, a villain flag, or a rescue quest.

It may involve control over money, housing, documents, medicine, body firmware, communication, transportation, children, reputation, air, heat, and social relationships.

The game must represent danger without teaching that outsiders always understand what the survivor should do.

## Core Principle

> **Safety matters. So do privacy, self-determination, material reality, and the survivor's right to make decisions that outsiders find frustrating.**

# 1. Abuse and Conflict Are Not the Same

Ordinary conflict may include anger, incompatibility, betrayal, poor communication, or separation.

Abuse involves patterns such as:

- coercive control;
- intimidation;
- surveillance;
- isolation;
- threats;
- physical harm;
- sexual coercion;
- financial control;
- reproductive coercion;
- control of documents or legal status;
- control of medication or assistive devices;
- deliberate sleep deprivation;
- destruction of property or source-chain records;
- threats toward children, dependents, animals, machines, or community members;
- manipulation of public reputation;
- exploiting life-support dependency.

One argument does not prove abuse. One calm public appearance does not disprove it.

# 2. Coercive Infrastructure

Symtropy's setting creates forms of control that contemporary domestic systems may not contain.

Examples include:

- locking a partner out of pressure-safe housing;
- controlling oxygen, water, heat, or charging access;
- revoking vehicle or route permissions;
- withholding body-firmware updates;
- controlling reconstitution credentials;
- confiscating a Field Deck;
- monitoring private messages through household systems;
- threatening to expose medical, sexual, migration, or source-chain information;
- forcing a machine partner into diagnostic mode;
- manipulating an alien partner's environmental needs;
- retaining custody of a shared symbiont or copied memory.

Infrastructure control must remain physically and legally grounded.

# 3. Survivor Agency

A survivor may:

- leave immediately;
- remain temporarily;
- conceal plans;
- seek partial help;
- refuse police or public hearings;
- return after leaving;
- protect someone else;
- prioritize children, medicine, housing, work, or immigration status;
- love the abusive person;
- distrust helpers;
- deny or minimize danger;
- be angry at intervention;
- pursue justice later;
- never pursue reconciliation.

The game does not punish a survivor for failing to follow the player's preferred plan.

# 4. Safety Planning

A credible escape requires resources and timing.

Possible needs include:

```text
safe communication
private evidence storage
money or transferable value
identity documents
medication and assistive devices
transport
shelter capacity
child or dependent plans
animal or machine care
work coverage
legal advice
trusted contacts
route knowledge
source-chain protection
```

Safety plans may fail or need revision.

A player can help create options without becoming the sole savior.

# 5. Confidential Services

Societies may provide:

- confidential clinics;
- shelters;
- mobile safe rooms;
- legal aid;
- document replacement;
- emergency transport;
- childcare;
- financial assistance;
- secure device repair;
- body-firmware support;
- machine diagnostic sanctuaries;
- migration advocacy;
- peer networks;
- culturally specific services.

Services have capacity limits, staff, rules, risks, and political enemies.

A shelter is not automatically safe for every species, gender, family, machine body, or immigration status.

# 6. Children and Dependents

Children and dependent people require protection without becoming leverage or plot devices.

The game must distinguish:

- immediate safety;
- guardianship;
- custody;
- supported decision-making;
- family separation;
- reunification;
- the child's own testimony and needs;
- institutional failure.

The player cannot use a child to force reconciliation, confession, or relationship continuation.

# 7. Evidence

Possible evidence includes:

- testimony;
- injuries;
- messages;
- access logs;
- financial records;
- device-control history;
- witness accounts;
- damaged property;
- medical records;
- threat patterns;
- route restrictions;
- source-chain interference.

Evidence has privacy and custody requirements.

IRIS may help preserve records but must not classify a person as an abuser from private behavioral prediction alone.

The absence of perfect evidence does not mean no safety action is possible. Safety and adjudication have different thresholds.

# 8. Institutional Response

Possible responses include:

- emergency separation;
- protective orders;
- access restrictions;
- alternative housing;
- supervised contact;
- employment protections;
- financial relief;
- criminal or civil proceedings;
- restorative processes where voluntarily chosen and safe;
- treatment programs;
- child-protection review;
- immigration protection;
- institutional reform.

The game should represent common failures:

- disbelief;
- retaliation;
- information leaks;
- economic dependence;
- biased enforcement;
- procedural delay;
- abusers manipulating reconciliation language;
- public institutions protecting prestigious people;
- communities pressuring survivors to preserve family reputation.

# 9. Accountability

Accountability may involve:

- restrictions;
- restitution;
- treatment;
- loss of authority;
- public or private findings;
- criminal sanction;
- employment consequences;
- repair of systems used for control;
- long-term monitoring under law;
- recognition of institutional complicity.

An abusive person may have trauma, addiction, illness, or a history of victimization. Those facts may matter to care and prevention. They do not erase responsibility or make reconciliation mandatory.

# 10. Community Complexity

Communities may respond through:

- denial;
- gossip;
- protection;
- informal warnings;
- religious mediation;
- worker solidarity;
- family pressure;
- victim blaming;
- practical support;
- vigilante action;
- institutional cover-up.

No community is one opinion.

A respected leader may be abusive privately. A disliked person may be falsely accused. A survivor may also have harmed others. The systems preserve evidence and safety without requiring a simple moral cast.

# 11. Player Boundaries

The player may:

- listen;
- provide transport or resources;
- protect evidence;
- repair a device;
- accompany someone;
- connect services;
- intervene in immediate danger;
- testify to witnessed facts;
- respect refusal;
- challenge institutional failure.

The player may not:

- purchase survivor loyalty;
- force disclosure;
- secretly install surveillance “for safety” without authority;
- compel reconciliation;
- demand gratitude;
- turn the survivor into a romance reward;
- infer guilt from a hidden game flag;
- resolve the entire pattern through one combat encounter.

# 12. Representation Rules

The game may depict:

- controlling behavior;
- fear;
- injury aftermath;
- broken property;
- financial and technological coercion;
- institutional failure;
- escape planning;
- ambivalence;
- separation;
- accountability;
- ordinary life after abuse.

It should avoid:

- graphic sexual violence;
- extended voyeuristic assault scenes;
- children harmed for shock;
- repeated threat escalation solely to motivate the player;
- “perfect victim” expectations;
- implying that survivors are permanently broken;
- romanticizing dangerous jealousy;
- using abuse as a shortcut to make every antagonist irredeemable.

# 13. Survivor Life Beyond Abuse

Every survivor character requires:

- work, interests, or projects;
- relationships unrelated to abuse;
- humor or ordinary habits;
- preferences;
- flaws;
- future choices;
- privacy;
- possible joy;
- the right not to become a public symbol.

Recovery may involve safety, grief, anger, boredom, financial rebuilding, new relationships, no new relationships, relocation, community, and changing self-understanding.

# 14. Multiplayer and Moderation

Player-on-player domestic or intimate coercion is not excused as roleplay.

Servers need:

- consent controls;
- privacy controls;
- reporting;
- blocking and separation;
- evidence preservation;
- moderator boundaries;
- anti-retaliation measures;
- content filters;
- safe exit from shared housing or organizations.

No player may use life-support, inventory, source-chain, or group authority to trap another player outside agreed competitive rules.

# 15. Acceptance Failures

This contract fails if:

- abuse is only visible assault;
- leaving requires no resources or risk;
- the survivor loses agency to the rescue plot;
- one dialogue choice creates safety;
- a hidden abuse score substitutes for evidence;
- private records become public automatically;
- children exist mainly as leverage;
- reconciliation is treated as the ideal ending;
- violence is romanticized as passion;
- the abusive person's trauma erases responsibility;
- the player is rewarded with intimacy for helping;
- systemic enablers disappear after one antagonist is removed.

## Closing Principle

> **The game goes there by showing how control enters rooms, accounts, devices, bodies, and relationships—and by preserving the survivor as a person whose life is larger than the abuse.**
