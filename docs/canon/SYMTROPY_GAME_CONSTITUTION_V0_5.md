# Symtropy Game Constitution

## One-Sentence Promise

**Symtropy is a first-person civilization sandbox in which players explore living worlds, survive their pressures, build physical systems, form societies, fight over futures, and preserve histories across divergent worldlines.**

## The Center of the Game

Symtropy is not fundamentally about water pumps, voting, factories, combat, aliens, or decentralization.

It is about **civilizational agency**:

```text
The player can enter a wounded world,
understand how it works,
change what it can become,
and live with the history created by that change.
```

Every major system must strengthen at least one part of that promise:

```text
enter
understand
change
become
remember
```

## The Eight Product Pillars

### 1. Embodied World

The player acts through a body in a physical place.

Weight, distance, weather, visibility, terrain, tools, vehicles, wounds, pressure, heat, gravity, and material condition matter. Menus may coordinate action, but they cannot replace the sensation of doing it.

### 2. Exploration and Discovery

The world must contain places, phenomena, ecologies, machines, histories, and forms of life that are worth seeking even when no quest marker points toward them.

Discovery is not only finding loot. It includes:

```text
a new route
a hidden process
an unknown species
a lost archive
a strange physical law
a surviving community
a planetary scar
an alien category of agency
```

### 3. Construction and Invention

Players do not merely restore the old world. They create new capability.

They build:

```text
tools
vehicles
habitats
factories
roads
farms
networks
robots
institutions
ships
cities
```

Construction should change movement, production, safety, culture, ecology, and strategic possibility.

### 4. Living Systems

Settlements, factions, ecologies, economies, machines, and cultures respond over time.

The game is not a static map with scripted quests. It is a coupled world where causes propagate, people interpret events, and repeated behavior creates institutions.

### 5. Conflict and Risk

Symtropy must support dangerous, skillful, exciting play.

Combat, hostile environments, raids, convoy defense, sabotage, pursuit, rescue, war, and first-contact escalation are legitimate parts of the game. Moral depth must not make conflict sluggish or tactically bland.

### 6. Society and Culture

Players build more than bases.

They participate in labor, trade, care, ritual, art, belief, governance, pleasure, education, mourning, celebration, and identity. Civilization must answer not only how people survive, but what survival is for.

### 7. Planetary and Cosmic Horizon

The first game begins locally, but the design horizon includes:

```text
whole regions
continents
oceans
orbital habitats
moons
planets
interplanetary logistics
alien contact
worldline diplomacy
interstellar seed civilizations
```

The horizon should be visible early without being prematurely implemented.

### 8. Durable History

Not every action becomes permanent, but meaningful outcomes do.

The Chronicle preserves:

```text
foundings
betrayals
discoveries
wars
treaties
extinctions
restorations
migrations
inventions
deaths
worldline forks
```

Players should be able to point at the world and say, “That exists because of what happened.”

## The Core Verb Constellation

Symtropy must support a broad constellation of verbs:

```text

climb
```

No single verb is the game.

A feature becomes central only when it combines with other verbs and changes the world.

## Scale Ladder

### Hand Scale

Tools, weapons, cables, samples, wounds, doors, machine panels.

### Site Scale

Ruins, caves, factories, habitats, ships, laboratories, temples, dungeons.

### Settlement Scale

Neighborhoods, workshops, farms, markets, power, water, care, culture, defense.

### Regional Scale

Roads, rivers, convoys, ecosystems, trade, migration, weather, warfare, communication.

### Planetary Scale

Climate, orbital access, continental politics, biospheres, large infrastructure, planetary history.

### Worldline Scale

Forks, migrations, confluence, durable identity, cross-world treaties, historical ancestry.

Every scale must have its own meaningful decisions. Larger scales may not erase the tactile reality of smaller ones.

## Player Fantasies

Symtropy should support players who primarily want to be:

```text
explorer
builder
engineer
driver
pilot
fighter
logistician
scientist
ecologist
medic
trader
leader
diplomat
archivist
artist
storyteller
```

A healthy session should let several fantasies cooperate without requiring every player to enjoy every system.

## The First Game: Seedworks

Seedworks is not a municipal utility simulator.

It is the first regional proof that Symtropy’s systems can coexist.

Firstlight Basin should contain:

```text
a settlement worth caring about
terrain worth exploring
infrastructure worth changing
danger worth preparing for
people with lives beyond the crisis
a machine ecology with tactical presence
a resource network with real logistics
a culture beginning to form
visible orbital and planetary horizons
more than one future
```

Water remains important because life depends on it, not because it defines the whole product.

## The Regional Proof Rule

Do not attempt the whole galaxy first.

Build one region where the whole game is already trying to emerge.

The first regional slice must demonstrate at least:

```text
embodied traversal
discovery
combat or meaningful danger
physical construction or repair
resource movement
a vehicle or mobility transition
living NPC response
one cultural moment
one persistent world change
one glimpse of the cosmic horizon
```

## Anti-Reduction Rules

Symtropy has failed its identity if players can accurately describe it as only:

```text
a pump repair game
a governance simulator
a factory builder
a survival shooter
a blockchain MMO
a solarpunk city builder
a philosophical walking simulator
a space engineering sandbox
```

It may contain all of these, but its identity comes from their integration.

## Fun Before Explanation

The player must be able to enjoy:

```text
moving through the world
operating tools
driving vehicles
solving physical problems
building systems
fighting threats
finding strange places
watching settlements change
```

before understanding the full political or historical meaning.

Meaning should deepen satisfying action, not substitute for it.

## Moral Texture Rule

Every serious conflict may contain understandable motives, but not every scene needs to be a formal ethical dilemma.

The world also needs:

```text
humor
vanity
rivalry
desire
boredom
curiosity
spectacle
pride
misunderstanding
recklessness
ordinary kindness
```

People must feel alive before they feel representative.

## Technology Rule

Technology is capability plus dependency.

Every meaningful technology should answer:

```text
What new action becomes possible?
What material and knowledge does it require?
What maintenance does it create?
What geography does it change?
Who gains power?
What new failure becomes possible?
```

Technology progression should open new scales of play rather than only improve numeric efficiency.

## Canonical Scope Gate

A proposed feature must pass four questions:

1. **Player action:** What does the player actually do?
2. **World consequence:** What changes outside the player inventory?
3. **System connection:** Which other pillar does it combine with?
4. **Production boundary:** What is the smallest convincing implementation?

Features that provide lore without action, simulation without readability, or complexity without new decisions should be deferred.

## Final Product Test

Symtropy succeeds when a player can tell a story like:

```text
We crossed a storm basin in a damaged crawler,
found a factory teaching itself to manufacture defensive organisms,
used its line to build a bridge instead,
angered the faction that claimed the factory,
saved a migrating settlement,
and years later the bridge had become the center of a city
whose children believed the rescue convoy was a founding myth.
```

That is the game.
