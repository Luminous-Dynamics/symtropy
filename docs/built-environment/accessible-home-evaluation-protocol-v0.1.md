# ACCESS-HOME-STUDY-001 — participatory longitudinal evaluation protocol v0.1

**Status:** research protocol proposal. Not ethics approval, clinical advice, building certification, or evidence that any intervention is effective or safe.

## 1. Purpose

Software fixtures can verify that a planner follows its own rules. They cannot establish that a real home supports a resident's life as intended. This protocol describes how future studies could evaluate accessibility interventions using resident-defined outcomes, direct task evidence, constraint tracking and repeated follow-up—without withholding necessary accommodations to manufacture a control group.

The unit of evaluation is a **resident-defined goal in a declared context**, not a diagnosis, room type, device or universal accessibility score. A successful outcome may include a service animal, human assistance, assistive technology or other support when that arrangement is what the resident chooses.

## 2. Research grounding and limits

The World Health Organization's *Housing and health guidelines* addresses accessibility for people with functional impairments alongside other housing-health risks. The accessibility systematic review bundled with the 2018 guideline included literature through April 2018. At the time, it noted relatively few high-quality studies, variation in interventions and measures, and a need for stronger standardized and longitudinal outcome research. It identified outcomes such as injury rates, well-being, quality of life, mental health, reliance on external care services and social participation. This is an evidence-gap signal from that review, not a claim that no newer research exists; conduct an updated literature search before field recruitment.

Newer reviews make the next research priorities more specific. A 2023 systematic review identified 12 randomized trials of home modifications for older adults and reported that occupational-therapist-led approaches combined with clinical, physical-activity or behavioral components were among the more successful models ([Sheth & Cogle, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36655622/)). A 2025 systematic review of 20 studies on home modifications for older adults identified gaps in personalization, smart-home technologies, long-term adaptation, social engagement and cost-effectiveness ([Cha, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40218050/)). Both reviews focus on older adults, so they do not establish general effects for every disability group, child or housing context. The 2025 review uses narrative synthesis and describes heterogeneous evidence; treat these findings as priorities for study design, not a universal effect estimate. The protocol therefore measures resident-defined agency and participation alongside technical task performance, support/caregiver burden, lifecycle cost, maintainability, privacy, and failure recovery. Refresh and document the search strategy and evidence appraisal before recruitment.

ISO 21542:2021 covers accessibility/usability of the built environment, including access, circulation, egress and fire evacuation; residential-unit recommendations are included. Its official status page currently identifies the 2021 edition as published and records a review milestone on 3 September 2026. Verify its latest lifecycle status, relevant editions and applicable legal requirements before using it in a live project.

South Africa's *National Strategic Framework on Universal Design and Access* provides important local policy context, but does not itself certify an individual building or intervention.

## 3. Non-negotiable ethical boundaries

1. **Resident partnership and compensation.** Disabled residents are co-designers, not merely test subjects. Budget for participation, preparation, access supports, travel, support persons where requested, and follow-up time.
2. **Ongoing accessible consent.** Consent materials and study interactions must be available in the participant's chosen accessible forms. Participants can pause, skip a task, withdraw, change their mind or decline recording without losing compensation already earned.
3. **No withheld necessary accommodation.** Do not withdraw, delay or randomize away an established or necessary accommodation to create a comparison group. If a configuration is already unsafe, record the barrier and remedy it; do not reproduce it as a baseline task.
4. **No forced independence.** A participant's preferred and available assistance can be part of success. Measure whether the chosen support works and is reliably available, not whether help was avoided.
5. **No unnecessary diagnosis or surveillance.** Describe goals, environments and access requirements without collecting diagnoses unless specifically necessary, consented and ethics-approved. No ambient cameras, microphones, continuous tracking or emotion inference as a default method. Store no raw video, identifiable floor plans or home addresses in a public fixture.
6. **Professional boundaries.** Researchers do not replace qualified building, accessibility, electrical, fire or life-safety professionals. Do not test smoke/fire alarms, egress under hazardous conditions, dangerous power failures or other life-safety failure modes except under an appropriate, qualified and approved safety protocol.
7. **Ethics and local review first.** Obtain the applicable ethics review, insurance/safety arrangements, site permission and professional review before field evaluation. This document is not that approval.

## 4. Study sequence

### Phase A — co-design and preregistration

- Invite participants with a range of access needs, including overlapping sensory, mobility, dexterity, fatigue/pain, cognitive/processing and communication needs. Do not treat one person's results as representative of a category.
- Let each participant define the goal, observable outcome, operating conditions, chosen supports and acceptable alternatives in their own terms.
- Register hard constraints separately from preferences. Record how the participant approved those distinctions and how they can revise them.
- Select the primary task outcomes, follow-up schedule, event definitions, analysis approach, data retention and stopping conditions before evaluating the intervention.
- Choose participant-appropriate rating instruments with qualified research input. Do not invent a universal subjective scale or compare unlike scales as if equivalent.

### Phase B — model and mock-up review

- Review the proposed path and alternative paths with the participant before modifying a home.
- Inspect the declared task sequence, controls, hand-offs, constraints, failure triggers and fallback options.
- Use a safe mock-up or walkthrough to find design conflicts early. Label assumptions, proposed evidence, unknowns and required site measurements explicitly.
- Let the participant select the modalities, equipment and assistance used during the mock-up. A model success is not a real-home outcome.

### Phase C — site review and implementation

- With consent, measure the relevant site and record the context, measurement method, date, reviewer role and uncertainty.
- Ask qualified local professionals to handle code, structural, electrical, fire/egress and other safety judgments within their competence.
- Record actual configuration and what was installed, adapted, funded, deferred or changed. Unimplemented recommendations are not outcome improvements.
- Do not mark a constraint satisfied solely because a product was purchased or a feature appeared on a plan.

### Phase D — task observations and follow-up

- Establish a safe baseline using the existing configuration and the participant's normal chosen supports. Never recreate an unsafe state or remove necessary support.
- Observe the agreed task in a way that minimizes intrusion. Allow the participant to stop or request help at any time.
- Test alternate or degraded paths only when the condition can be introduced safely, consented to and approved by relevant professionals. If a condition is unsafe to simulate, document it as untested and use a separate expert-led risk process.
- Revisit the outcome after implementation. A suggested schedule is baseline, an early post-implementation check, 1 month, 3 months and 6 months; an optional 12-month check can examine sustained effects. Adapt this schedule collaboratively; these are protocol proposals, not validated universal intervals.
- Capture whether the environment, device, support availability, health/functioning context or the participant's priorities changed between measurements.

## 5. Outcome vector

Do not compress these dimensions into a single accessibility score. Report the goal, context, denominator, time point, missingness and uncertainty with each outcome.

| Dimension | Measure | Interpretation guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Resident goal attainment | Participant-approved observable goal achieved, partially achieved or not achieved | Define the criterion before evaluation; retain narrative context |
| Hard constraints | Each constraint: satisfied with evidence, conflicted, unresolved or not applicable | A hard conflict cannot be averaged away |
| Task completion | Outcome, assistance used, whether that support was chosen/available, and relevant task conditions | Assistance is not failure if it is part of the participant's goal |
| Fallback and recovery | Whether the planned alternate path worked under the declared safe test condition; recovery time where meaningful | Untested is not passed; never stage hazardous failures |
| Blockers and safety signals | Barriers, mistakes, near misses, adverse events, interruptions and the action taken | Record negative outcomes and stop when needed |
| Participant experience | Participant-chosen effort, autonomy, privacy, confidence, acceptability and satisfaction indicators | Use chosen/appropriate measures; do not force one scale across people |
| Practical burden | Out-of-pocket cost, implementation time, upkeep, energy/network dependence and support burden where relevant | Separate paid/unpaid effort and resident/carer perspectives |
| Equity and accessibility | Results by task and relevant intersection of access requirements where adequately sampled | Small cohorts require careful, often descriptive reporting; do not overgeneralize |
| Evidence quality | Known, proposed, unknown, measured, professionally reviewed; date, source and scope | Missingness and confidence are reported, not silently imputed |

Where a measure needs independent observation, obtain participant consent and make observation roles explicit. Inter-rater reliability can be assessed for appropriate observable measures, but is not proof that the measure represents the participant's priorities.

## 6. Proposed qualification targets

These are process and integrity targets, not reported performance.

- **100%** of evaluated goals and acceptable supports are defined or approved by the participant before task evaluation.
- **100%** of findings include evidence source/reference, context, date/scope and reviewer role—or are marked unknown with the reason recorded.
- **100%** of applicable hard constraints have an explicit disposition; no missing or unverified hard constraint is represented as satisfied.
- **100%** of known hard conflicts trigger remediation or prevent that path being counted as a successful candidate.
- **100%** of preference conflicts and unresolved preferences remain visible in participant/reviewer reports.
- **100%** of study runs report denominator, missed visits, missing data, withdrawals and changes in intervention.
- **0** unconsented personal data collection, covert surveillance, unsafe tests introduced for research convenience, or necessary accommodations withheld to create a comparator.
- **0** claims of real-home qualification based only on schema validation, model output or synthetic fixtures.
- Numeric task success and recovery thresholds are set with participants before each study. Avoid universal claims such as "90% accessible" without a defined population, task, design, measurement validity and acceptable failure consequence.
- All adverse events and significant negative findings receive documented review and disposition; publication reports limitations alongside positive outcomes.

## 7. Analysis and reporting

- Register primary outcomes, analysis choices, inclusion criteria, exclusions, missing-data rules, stopping rules and intended subgroup descriptions before observing outcomes.
- Report goal-level results and the count of hard constraints satisfied, conflicted and unresolved. Do not let a mean task completion rate hide a participant whose chosen goal is blocked.
- Report estimates with uncertainty intervals when methods and sample size support them. Explain when a sample supports only qualitative or descriptive conclusions; do not manufacture significance from a small cohort.
- For repeated observations, preselect a method appropriate to the design and outcome. Consider within-participant trajectories and contextual changes, not just an aggregate before/after score.
- If multiple adaptation components are evaluated, preregister which effects can reasonably be attributed to which components. The WHO guideline notes the value of stronger designs, including component analysis where feasible; ethical constraints may preclude randomization or withholding an intervention.
- Publish protocol deviations, adverse outcomes, missingness, costs, non-implementation and the range of resident preferences. Share only consented, carefully de-identified data; raw plans or detailed home layouts can re-identify people.

## 8. Data and evidence handling

Keep a provenance record for every finding: stable pseudonymous goal/constraint identifier, date, context/condition, data source, measurement method, reviewer role, status, uncertainty, and change history. Minimize personal information. Restrict raw site records to the agreed access group; use synthetic examples for public software tests. A cryptographic digest proves which bytes were processed, not that the evidence is true.

## 10. Open questions for co-design and expert review

- Which participant-selected outcome measures are valid, accessible and useful for different goals and contexts?
- What follow-up intervals are feasible without imposing excessive burden?
- Which real-world fallback tests are safe and meaningful versus better handled by professional review or non-hazardous simulation?
- How should the study budget compensate participants for time, expertise, access costs and co-authorship?
- How can evaluations include low-income, rental, rural and low-resource contexts without assuming that expensive technology is available or maintainable?
- How should researchers protect sensitive site records while making aggregate evidence reproducible?

No answer should be finalized solely by the software maintainers: include residents, accessibility practitioners, built-environment professionals, research-methods expertise, and applicable local ethics/jurisdictional reviewers.
